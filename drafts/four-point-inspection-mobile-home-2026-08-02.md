# The 4 Point Inspection for Mobile Homes: How to Pass the First Time

You've got 30 days before your insurance renewal, and your agent just dropped the news: no updated inspection report, no policy. If you own a mobile home in Florida, that phone call usually means one thing, a 4 point inspection. Mobile home owners around Ocala hear this every week, and the ones who scramble at the last minute almost always pay more than the ones who prep.

Here's the good news. A 4 point inspection on a mobile home is very passable, and most of what inspectors flag can be fixed in a day or two if you know it's coming. This guide walks through exactly what gets checked, why mobile homes fail more often than site-built houses, and the specific prep work that gets Marion County homes through on the first try.

## What a 4 Point Inspection Covers on a Mobile Home

The name tells you most of it. A licensed inspector looks at four systems: roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. Insurance companies use the report to decide whether they'll write or renew a policy on an older home, and in Florida that usually means anything 30 years old or more, though some carriers ask at 20 years or even sooner for manufactured homes.

For a mobile home, each of those four points has its own quirks:

**Roof.** The inspector wants to know the covering type, its age, and whether it has visible damage or active leaks. Metal roofovers are common on older single wides around here, and inspectors will note rust, loose fasteners, and ponding. Most carriers want at least 3 to 5 years of remaining life.

**Electrical.** Panel brand, wiring type, and amperage. Certain older panels get automatic red flags, and aluminum branch wiring is a frequent problem in homes built in the early 1970s. Pre-1976 homes, built before the HUD code took effect, get extra scrutiny across the board. You can read the federal standards that changed everything on the [HUD Manufactured Housing Program page](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/rmra/mhs/mhshome).

**Plumbing.** Supply line material matters a lot. Polybutylene, the gray plastic pipe used heavily in manufactured homes from the late 1970s through the mid 1990s, is an instant problem for many carriers. Inspectors also look for active leaks, water heater age, and signs of past water damage.

**HVAC.** Age and condition of your heating and cooling. A working central system, even an older one, usually passes. Window units alone can be an issue with some carriers.

Here's what surprises most mobile home owners: even though the inspection is officially about those four systems, the inspector is walking around and under your home with a camera. Sagging floors, a home that's visibly out of level, rusted tie-downs, and standing water under the belly all end up in the photos. Those photos shape how the underwriter reads the whole report.

## Why Mobile Homes Fail the 4 Point Inspection

Mobile homes fail at a higher rate than site-built homes, and it's rarely because the furnace died. The most common failure points we see in Marion County reports:

1. **Polybutylene or galvanized plumbing.** The single biggest killer on older manufactured homes.
2. **Outdated or flagged electrical panels.** Zinsco, Federal Pacific, and certain fuse boxes.
3. **Roof at end of life.** Especially rolled roofing and aged metal roofovers.
4. **Water damage evidence.** Stains, soft subfloor, torn belly board. Inspectors photograph it even when the leak was fixed years ago.
5. **Structural notes.** Out-of-level frames, failing piers, missing skirting, and corroded anchors get written up as supporting comments, and underwriters read them.

That last category is where we spend most of our time. A 4 point inspection for a mobile home technically isn't a foundation inspection, but Florida underwriters treat manufactured homes as higher risk to begin with, so any hint of settlement or tie-down failure can push a "pass with notes" into a decline. If your doors have started sticking or you've noticed cracks creeping across the drywall, deal with it before the inspection, not after. ## The Structure Question: Piers, Level, and Tie-Downs

Let's talk about the part of the report that isn't one of the four points but sinks more mobile home policies than people realize.

Florida insurers care about wind. A lot. Many carriers require proof of proper tie-downs on manufactured homes, and some ask for a separate tie-down certification alongside the 4 point. Marion County sits in Wind Zone 2 under the HUD map, which sets minimum anchoring requirements for newer homes, and older homes are often grandfathered with fewer anchors than they should have. If your straps are rusted through or your anchors have pulled loose in our sandy soil, that's a real safety issue and an insurance issue at the same time. We covered how the paperwork side works in [tie-down certification and insurance](/blog/tie-down-certification-insurance/).

The level of the home matters too, for a sneaky reason. An out-of-level frame stresses everything the inspector is actually grading. Doors rack, plumbing joints pull, roof seams open up, and ductwork separates at the crossover. So a home that's 3 inches low on one corner doesn't just get a structural note, it often fails on a "point" that the settling caused.

## How to Prep Before the Inspector Shows Up

You can knock out a surprising amount of this yourself in a weekend. Here's the prep list we give customers:

**Two weeks out:**

- Find your paperwork. Roof permits, HVAC install receipts, water heater age, any prior tie-down certificates. Documented ages beat guessed ages every time.
- Walk the roof line from the ground. Look for lifted edges, rust streaks, and sagging.
- Open your electrical panel door and photograph the label. Search the brand. If it's on a carrier blacklist, price a replacement now, not after a failed report.
- Run every faucet and look under every sink with a flashlight. A $6 supply line fixes a "active leak" note that could tank the report.

**One week out:**

- Pull a few skirting panels and look underneath. You're checking for standing water, hanging belly board, leaning piers, and daylight where straps should be. Our [underside inspection checklist](/blog/mobile-home-underside-inspection-checklist/) walks through it panel by panel.
- Test every window and door. Sticking doors suggest settlement, and inspectors notice.
- Replace HVAC filters and clear debris from around the condenser. Cosmetic? Sure. But a maintained-looking system reads as a maintained home.

**Day of:**

- Clear access to the panel, water heater, attic hatch, and crawl space entry.
- Have your documents in one folder.
- Don't hover, but be available for questions.

What you should not do is crawl under the home and start jacking on piers yourself. Lifting a mobile home without the right equipment and blocking is genuinely dangerous, and a botched DIY relevel shows up in inspection photos looking worse than the original settlement. Check pier condition, note problems, then hand the actual lifting to someone insured for it.

## When to Call a Pro (and What to Ask)

Some of this list is homeowner territory. Filters, supply lines, paperwork, clearing access. Go for it.

Call a professional when the problem involves:

- **Anything load-bearing.** Piers, jacks, frame, blocking. One slipped jack can total a home or worse.
- **Tie-downs and anchors.** Florida has specific requirements, and torque, angle, and soil condition all matter. Guessing gets you a failed re-inspection.
- **Panel work or repiping.** Licensed trades only, both for safety and because insurers want permits and receipts.
- **Anything under a home with standing water or sagging insulation.** You don't know what's soaked until it's opened up.

When you're hiring, ask whether the contractor is licensed and insured, whether the estimate is written, and whether they'll document the work with photos you can hand to your insurer. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a 4 point inspection on a mobile home check the foundation and tie-downs?

Not officially, the four points are roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. But inspectors photograph obvious settlement, failing piers, and rusted straps as supporting notes, and underwriters absolutely read them. Many Florida carriers also require a separate tie-down certification for manufactured homes, so treat the structure as part of the test even though it isn't one of the four points.

### How old does my mobile home have to be before insurers require a 4 point inspection?

Most Florida carriers ask for one at 30 years, and many require it sooner for manufactured homes, often at 20 years or at any policy change. Pre-1976 homes built before the HUD code face the toughest underwriting no matter what the report says. Ask your agent for the specific trigger age, since it varies by company.

### How much does a 4 point inspection cost, and how long does it take?

In the Ocala area, expect roughly $75 to $150 for the inspection itself, and it usually takes 30 to 60 minutes on site. That's the cheap part. The real cost question is the repairs it uncovers, which is why a pre-inspection walkthrough of your own home is worth an afternoon.

### What happens if my mobile home fails the 4 point inspection?

You'll get the report listing the deficiencies, and your carrier will either decline, non-renew, or give you a window to fix and re-inspect. Fix the flagged items, keep receipts and photos, and order a re-inspection. Most structural fixes, like releveling or anchor replacement, take a single day, so a fail is usually a delay rather than a dead end.
