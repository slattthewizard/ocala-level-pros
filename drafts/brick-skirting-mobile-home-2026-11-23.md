# Brick Skirting for a Mobile Home: Footings, Vents, and the Leveling Problem

Brick skirting for a mobile home is a real masonry wall that closes in the space under the home, and it is the longest-lasting way to do it. It is also a different kind of job than snapping in panels. The wall needs its own footing, vents, and an access door, and it cannot touch the home's weight. Build it around a home that is still settling and one of the two will lose.

We do not do masonry. We install vinyl and rigid-foam skirting, and we level homes. So this is not a bid. It is what we would want a homeowner to know before hiring a mason, because the wall changes how the home can be serviced for as long as it stands.

## Brick, Block, and Cinder Block: What You Are Buying

The names overlap, so ask what you are being quoted.

- **Brick skirting.** A brick wall, usually one wythe thick, laid on a footing. The most finished look.
- **Concrete block or cinder block skirting.** Hollow block laid on a footing, often with a stucco, paint, or brick-veneer face. Cheaper and faster than full brick. Cinder block is the old name for block made with cinders, and most block sold now is concrete.
- **Faux brick or stone panels.** Foam or polymer panels with a brick or stone pattern. These are skirting panels, not masonry. We install the rigid-foam version, and our post on [faux stone skirting for mobile homes](/blog/faux-stone-skirting-mobile-home/) covers it.

When someone says concrete block underpinning, they mean the block wall. For the wider comparison, see [mobile home skirting options](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-options/).

## The Wall Needs Its Own Footing

Masonry is heavy. A wall of brick or block set directly on bare sand will settle, crack, and lean, and Marion County sand shifts and washes out under heavy rain.

A proper masonry wall stands on its own footing, a poured concrete base set below the surface and sized by whoever designs it. That footing runs around the home's perimeter, outside the piers and pads that hold the home. The footing does one job: carry the wall. It does not carry the home.

A mason or an engineer sets the depth and width, and soil and local building requirements decide what is needed. Ask for it in writing. If a quote says skirting wall with no mention of a footing, that is a red flag.

Because a footing and a built wall are permanent changes, check whether your county or park requires a permit or approval before work starts. If you are wondering whether a wall and footing turn the home into a permanent foundation setup, our post on [permanent foundation vs piers](/blog/permanent-foundation-vs-piers/) covers what that term means and what it costs.

## A Curtain Wall, Not a Foundation

The wall is a curtain wall. It closes in the space. The home's weight goes down through its piers, and the tie-downs hold it to the ground. Our post on [mobile home pier types](/blog/mobile-home-pier-types/) shows what those piers are.

That sounds simple, and it is where masonry jobs go wrong. If the wall is built tight against the underside of the home, the home has nowhere to go when it settles, and it sits on the wall. If it is built with a gap at the top and a flexible closure, the home can move a bit and the wall stays put.

HUD's installation rules ask the same of any skirting. It must not be attached in a way that impedes the contraction and expansion of the home's exterior covering, and it must not trap water between the siding and trim ([24 CFR 3285.504](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.504)). A mason should build the top of the wall so it does neither.

## Vents and Access Door in Masonry

Federal installation rules apply to masonry too. A crawlspace with skirting must have ventilation openings, with a net area of at least one square foot for every 150 square feet of the home's floor area. That can be reduced to one square foot for every 1,500 square feet when a vapor retarder covers the ground under the whole home. Openings go on at least two opposite sides, as high as practicable, covered with a rodent-proof, corrosion-resistant covering ([24 CFR 3285.505](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505)).

Masonry makes two things harder:

- **Vents have to be built in.** Vent blocks or open course gaps go in during construction. Cutting vents into a finished wall is slow and messy. Our post on [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/) explains how many a home needs.
- **The access opening has to be framed in from the start.** The same section requires an access opening of at least 18 inches wide, 24 inches high, and three square feet, located so the utility connections under the home can be reached. On a block wall, that means a framed door or a removable section planned before the first course is laid.

Ask where the door goes and whether a person can crawl through it. A small hatch is not enough.

## Masonry and Releveling

This is the part most people never hear until it is a problem.

A mobile home settles. Sandy soil, heavy summer rain, and sinking piers move it over the years. When that happens, a crew levels it. We do this work, and it is done from underneath with jacks, shims, and new piers or pads.

Masonry changes that in three ways:

1. **It does not move with the home.** Vinyl and foam give a little. Brick and block do not, so a home that drops or lifts leaves a gap, or loads the wall, and the mortar cracks.
2. **Access.** A leveling crew has to get to every pier. A wall with one small door and piers far from it makes the job slower and harder, and in some layouts the crew cannot reach a pier at all without removing part of the wall.
3. **Lifting a home needs room.** A home that has dropped a half inch cannot be raised back up if the top of the wall is tight against the underside.

The sequence matters. Level the home first and fix the piers and anchors. Then build the wall. If a home has been settling for years, a masonry wall around it is the worst time to find out it still is. ## Termites and Pests

Masonry does not feed termites, and it stops most animals, which is a big plus. A few cautions:

- **Termites build mud tubes up masonry.** They travel through cracks and over the face, so a wall can hide a path to the wood above. Keep the footing and wall inspected and keep soil and mulch from piling against the wood.
- **Wood in contact with soil.** Any wood skirting or trim within 6 inches of the ground must be pressure-treated or naturally resistant to decay and termites ([24 CFR 3285.504](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.504)).
- **Vent screens.** The vents must be covered with a rodent-proof screen, so check it each year. Pest control is not our trade, so call a licensed pest company for termite treatment and inspection.

A masonry wall also keeps you from seeing the underside. Open the access door at least once a year and look at the belly, the piers, the anchors, and the ground barrier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does brick skirting need a footing?

Yes. A brick or block wall is heavy and needs its own poured footing, sized and set by the mason or an engineer. Without one, the wall settles and cracks. The footing carries the wall only, not the home.

### Can brick skirting hold up a mobile home?

No. It is a curtain wall that closes in the space. The home rests on its piers and is held down by its tie-downs. If the wall is built tight against the home, the home can settle onto it and the wall can crack.

### Does masonry skirting need vents and an access door?

Yes. Federal installation rules require ventilation openings sized by floor area, on at least two opposite sides, and an access opening at least 18 inches wide, 24 inches high, and three square feet. On masonry both have to be planned into the wall from the start.

### How does a block wall affect releveling?

It makes it harder. The wall does not move with the home, and a leveling crew needs access to every pier. A home that settles or needs lifting can load the wall or crack the mortar, so level the home first and leave a way to reach the piers.
