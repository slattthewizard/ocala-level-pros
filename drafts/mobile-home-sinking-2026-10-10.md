# Mobile Home Sinking on One Side: Why It Happens and How It Gets Releveled

If one side of your mobile home is sinking, the cause is almost always underneath it: a footing that washed out or sank, a pier that cracked or tipped, or shims that rotted. The home itself is rarely the problem. The fix is a careful relevel that rebuilds the failed support and then raises the home in small steps, not one big lift.

Where the drop shows up tells you a lot. One end, one side, the middle, and the marriage line each point to a different set of likely causes. Below are those patterns, how a crew diagnoses them, what the repair sequence looks like, and the cases where what feels like sinking is really a rotted floor.

## Start with the pattern, not the crack

You usually notice sinking through the house, not the ground: a door that swings open on its own, a cabinet gap that is wider on one end, a marble that rolls to one wall. Those symptoms are covered in our guide to the signs a mobile home needs leveling. Here the question is narrower: which part of the home is dropping, and why there?

Note whether the change was sudden (after a storm or a leak) or gradual, and whether the low area sits near a downspout, an AC drain line, a hose bib, or the edge of the roof. Water is the usual suspect, and it follows predictable paths.

## One Side Sinking

When one side of a mobile home is sinking, the first thing to check is where water lands on that side. The usual culprits:

A washed-out or undersized footing. Every pier is supposed to sit on its own footing, set on undisturbed soil or on fill compacted to 90 percent of maximum density, according to [24 CFR 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312). Florida sand is forgiving until water starts moving through it. A downspout that dumps beside the home, an AC condensate line that drips in one spot all summer, or a roofline that sheds water along one wall can wash fines out from under a footing and let it settle.

Rotted wood shims. Wood shims between the pier cap and the steel frame hold the final height. In a damp crawl space they soften and compress. One rotted stack lets that corner drop, and the home quietly follows.

A cracked or tipped pier. Concrete block piers crack when they carry uneven load, and a pier that leans has lost its ability to hold weight straight down. Our post on [when to replace mobile home piers](/blog/when-to-replace-mobile-home-piers/) explains how to tell a pier that needs a shim from one that needs to be rebuilt.

Pads on unconsolidated fill. If part of the lot was filled or regraded before the home was set and the fill was never properly compacted, the pads on that part will keep settling after the home is leveled. This is why a home can look fine for two years and then start leaning on one side.

Standing or running water under the home. Even if the footing was fine, a low spot that holds water will soften the soil beneath it. If you can see wet ground or a damp smell under the skirting, read our guide on [water under a mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/) first, because fixing the pier without fixing the water means doing the job twice.

## One End Sinking

When one end of the home drops, the pattern usually comes from a different set of causes than a drop along one side.

Common causes are a pier line set on weaker soil, a drain leaking at that end, and a downspout soaking the corner.

A heavy attached porch or addition that is not independently supported can also push the nearest piers down. If the sinking end lines up with one, tell the crew.

## The Middle Sagging

A mobile home sagging in the middle is a different animal. The steel chassis has two long I-beams running end to end, and the perimeter may be fine while the center drops.

A center sag usually means the piers along the I-beams are spaced too far apart, are missing in places, or have sunk unevenly, so the beams bow between supports. Correct spacing depends on the manufacturer installation manual and the roof load, so we read the manual and compare it with what is actually under the home.

For a double-wide, the center line carries more than people realize. Federal installation rules require center beam and mating wall support for multi-section homes ([24 CFR 3285.303(c)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.303)). If piers along that line were skipped, spaced too wide, or have settled, you get a mobile home center beam sagging, usually with a visible dip along the ceiling or floor that runs the length of the home, and often with a crack or a gap at the marriage line.

A sag that develops slowly over years often points to piers that were never right. A sag that appeared after a leak or a wet season points to soil or shims on that line.

## The Marriage Line Dropping

On a double-wide, one half can sink relative to the other. You will see it as a step in the floor at the seam, trim that has pulled apart, or a ceiling line that no longer lines up. The cause is usually a pier line under one half that has settled more than the other, often because one side of the lot drains differently.

Lifting one half quickly can pop the seam and split the trim, so a double-wide relevel is slower by design.

## What about a sinkhole?

Owners in Marion County hear the word sinkhole the moment something drops. A true sinkhole is rare compared with the everyday causes above, and most sinking traces back to those.

Some signs do point to one: a round depression in the yard, cracks that keep widening, doors and windows jamming across the whole home, or ground that drops suddenly. Read our guide to [sinkhole signs in Marion County](/blog/sinkhole-signs-marion-county/) for the full list. If you see them, do not schedule a relevel first. ## Why homes settle here in the first place

Sandy soil and heavy summer rain move the ground under piers over time. We keep the full explanation in [why mobile homes settle in Florida](/blog/why-mobile-homes-settle-florida/). Sinking is that same process, only faster or more local.

## How We Diagnose It

A good diagnosis answers three questions: how far off is the home, where is it off, and why.

Measure the whole frame. We shoot a laser or run a water level across the frame so every point reads against the same reference. A bubble level on one floor tells you about one room. A reference across the frame tells you the shape of the drop: a tilt, a bow, a twist, or a dip.

Check every pier, one at a time. We look at the footing under it, the pier stack, the cap, and the shims. Is the footing cracked or tilted? Is the pier plumb? Are the shims dry or soft? Is a pier missing or loose in its seat? We compare what is there with what the manufacturer manual calls for.

Look for the water. We check downspouts, AC drains, grading, and the soil under the low area.

The inspection is $150-$250 and covers the full underside check and a written report. The estimate for the repair itself is free.

## The Repair Sequence

A safe relevel runs in this order, whether the job is one corner or the whole home.

1. Fix the support first. Failed footings get replaced or reset on undisturbed soil or compacted fill, cracked or tipped piers are rebuilt, and rotted shims come out. Raising a home onto a bad footing just resets the clock.
2. Jack in small increments. The crew raises the low area a little at a time, checking the interior as they go, and never lifts one point far ahead of its neighbors. The goal is to move the frame, not twist it.
3. Shim to the final height. Once the frame reads level against the reference, solid shims go in between the pier cap and the steel, and the load is transferred back onto the piers.
4. Recheck the home itself. Doors and windows should open and close and the gaps at cabinets and trim should look right. We walk it with you.
5. Re-tension the straps. Raising or lowering a home changes strap tension. After a relevel, tie-down straps need to be checked and re-tensioned, and any rusted anchor or strap gets flagged. Our [tie-down and anchor service](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/) covers that side if the hardware needs work.
6. Fix the water. Redirect the downspout, move the AC drain, or regrade where needed, so the same footing does not wash out again.

Prices for the leveling work itself are a single-corner relevel at $300-$600, a full single-wide relevel at $450-$900, and a full double-wide relevel at $700-$1,500. Pier and footing repair is quoted on what we find under the home, which is why a written estimate comes first.

## Why fast jacking is a bad idea

Lifting a home quickly turns a sinking problem into a repair bill.

A mobile home's walls, ceiling and floor ride on the steel frame. If one corner is lifted faster than the rest, the frame twists. Drywall cracks at corners and door heads, windows bind, trim pulls apart, and on a double-wide the marriage line can pop.

A homeowner with a bottle jack and a stack of blocks runs the same risk. A slow, staged lift costs more time and prevents damage that costs far more to repair.

## When it is not a leveling job

Sometimes the floor feels like it is sinking when the frame has not moved at all. A spot that gives underfoot, a bounce near a bath or kitchen, or a soft patch by the water heater can mean rotted decking or damaged floor joists, usually from a slow plumbing or roof leak. A relevel will not fix that.

If a level reading across the frame comes out fine but the floor still dips, the floor itself has failed. Our guide on [soft spots in a mobile home floor](/blog/soft-spots-mobile-home-floor/) explains how to tell the difference and what repair looks like. We do not do floor or subfloor carpentry, so a rotted floor is a job for a carpenter, done before or alongside any leveling. Also rule out a leaking pipe under the home that is softening the soil, and a torn belly board hanging low.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is one side of my mobile home sinking?

Usually because a footing under that side washed out or settled, a pier cracked or tipped, or wood shims rotted. Water is the common thread: a downspout, AC drain or roofline dumping near that side softens the soil under the footing. A crew has to look at each pier to know which one it is.

### Is a sinking mobile home a sign of a sinkhole?

Rarely. Most sinking comes from ordinary support failures like a washed-out footing or a bad shim. A sinkhole shows up as a round depression, fast-widening cracks, or ground that drops suddenly. If you see those, call a Florida-licensed engineer before any leveling work.

### How much does it cost to fix a sinking mobile home?

Leveling work runs $300-$600 for a single-corner relevel, $450-$900 for a full single-wide, and $700-$1,500 for a full double-wide. The underside inspection is $150-$250. Replacing failed piers or footings is quoted from what the crew finds, and the written estimate is free.

### Can a mobile home sagging in the middle be fixed?

Often yes. A center sag usually comes from I-beam piers that are missing, too far apart, or settled, and the fix is to add or rebuild support along the beam line and raise it slowly. On a double-wide, the center line needs proper support under federal installation rules. If the floor itself is soft, the problem is rot, not leveling.
