# Mobile Home Crawl Space Encapsulation: Why Vents and a Vapor Barrier Come First

Mobile home crawl space encapsulation, in the sense of sealing the vents and closing the space up like a conditioned basement, is usually not how mobile homes are set up. HUD's installation standards call for ventilation openings in a skirted crawlspace and a vapor retarder on the ground. What does work is a full ground barrier, a sound belly wrap, good drainage and clear vents.

You will see encapsulation sold online, mostly for site-built houses. A mobile home sits on a different structure with a different moisture path, so the same product does not translate. This post explains the rule, why sealing up can backfire here, and what to do instead.

## What Encapsulation Means on a Regular House

On a site-built house, an encapsulated crawl space has foundation walls. Installers seal the vents, run heavy plastic up the walls and across the floor, and often add a dehumidifier or tie the space into the home's conditioned air. The space becomes part of the building envelope.

That works because the walls are solid masonry and the floor above is tight. A mobile home skirt is not a foundation wall. It is a light-duty enclosure hung on the home's frame, and the floor system above is covered by a belly wrap that is not an airtight seal. Trying to turn that into a conditioned space means a lot of tape, a lot of money, and a moisture problem waiting at every seam.

## What HUD's Installation Standards Say

Two sections of 24 CFR Part 3285 matter here.

**Ground moisture control.** If the space under the home is enclosed with skirting or other materials, a vapor retarder must cover the ground under the home, unless the home is in an arid region with dry soil conditions. The minimum is six mil polyethylene sheeting or equivalent, and the entire area under the home must be covered, except for areas under open porches, decks and recessed entries. Joints must overlap at least 12 inches ([24 CFR 3285.204](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.204)).

**Crawlspace ventilation.** A crawlspace with skirting must be provided with ventilation openings. The minimum net vent area is one square foot for every 150 square feet of floor area. That can drop to one square foot for every 1,500 square feet where a uniform 6-mil polyethylene sheet or other acceptable vapor retarder is installed on the ground under the entire floor area. Openings go as high as practicable, on at least two opposite sides, covered with a perforated corrosion and weather-resistant covering designed to prevent the entry of rodents ([24 CFR 3285.505](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505)).

Read those together. The standard lets you cut the required vent area when you have a ground barrier. It does not offer an exception that lets you close the vents altogether. The text says nothing about sealing the space, and it sets a minimum for the openings.

A caution. These are HUD's rules for installing a home, and they are not a full code for every setup. Your park, lender, insurer or local building department may have its own requirements, and a home on a permanent foundation can be treated differently. If you are thinking of sealing a crawl space, check with the authority that applies to your home first.

## Why Sealing a Mobile Home Crawl Space Can Backfire

Without ventilation, any moisture that gets under a mobile home stays there. Several things add moisture in Florida:

- **Ground moisture.** Soil gives off water vapor all year, and summer rain keeps it high.
- **Water from the home.** A leaking drain, supply line, water heater or AC condensate line puts water straight into the crawlspace.
- **Humid air.** Marion County air is damp for much of the year, and warm humid air meeting a cooler floor condenses.

Vents let some of that out. Seal them and the humidity builds, the belly insulation soaks, the subfloor stays damp, and mold and rot follow. Our post on [mold under a mobile home](/blog/mold-under-mobile-home/) covers what that looks like once it starts. A tight space with a hidden leak is worse than a vented one, because you will not notice the leak until the floor goes soft.

## What Does Work: The Dry Crawl Space Setup

You can get most of what encapsulation promises, a dry and clean crawl space, without sealing it. Here is the setup that works:

1. **Full ground vapor barrier.** Six mil polyethylene or better covering the whole ground area, joints overlapped, torn spots patched, cut around piers and anchors. Our post on the [best vapor barrier for a mobile home](/blog/best-vapor-barrier-mobile-home/) compares materials.
2. **Intact belly wrap.** The belly is the covering across the underside of the floor. Tears let in rain, animals and humidity, and let insulation fall. Patches or full replacement fix this.
3. **Open, screened vents.** Keep the skirting vents clear of dirt, mulch, shrubs and stored items, with screens that stop rodents. Our post on [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/) covers how many you need.
4. **Drainage.** Gutters and downspouts that send water away, and soil graded so rain runs off the pad rather than under the home.
5. **Plumbing and condensate kept tight.** Leaks under the home put water on the ground faster than any barrier can handle. Plumbing and AC repair belong to plumbers and HVAC techs, not us.
6. **A look underneath every year.** Check the barrier, the belly and the vents after the rainy season.

None of that needs a dehumidifier or a sealed envelope, and each piece is something a mobile home setup is already supposed to have.

## Ground Barrier: The Part That Does the Most

Of all the pieces above, the ground barrier usually does the most work. Bare sand and clay release water vapor into the space all day, and the barrier stops most of it from rising.

It has to be done right to work. A barrier with big gaps, loose edges or tears is not much better than none. Foot traffic, plumbing repairs and animals disturb it, and standing water on top of it just sits there. If you see plastic that is bunched, ripped, buried in sand or missing in sections, it needs fixing.

## Belly Wrap and Insulation

The belly wrap is the other half of the moisture story. It holds up the insulation and keeps rain, animals and humid air off the floor. If it sags, tears or has holes, moisture and critters get to the insulation and the underside of the floor.

Fixing the belly and the ground barrier together is better than doing one alone. A new ground barrier under a torn belly just means water gets in from above instead of below. ## Signs Your Crawl Space Is Too Wet

Look for any of these before you pay for anything:

- A musty smell inside the home, especially near floor vents.
- Soft or springy spots in the floor.
- Wet or sagging belly insulation, or a belly wrap that bulges.
- Standing water, damp soil or white mineral crust on the ground or piers.
- Mold or mildew on the framing, belly board or the back of the skirting.
- Rust on anchors, straps and pier hardware.

If you see these, find the water source first. A barrier on top of a leak is a waste. Our [underside inspection checklist](/blog/mobile-home-underside-inspection-checklist/) goes through what to look at, and always stay out from under a home that is supported only by a jack.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you encapsulate a mobile home crawl space?

Not in the way it is done on a site-built house. HUD's installation standards call for ventilation openings in a skirted crawlspace and a vapor retarder on the ground. A ground barrier is allowed to reduce the required vent area, but the vents themselves stay. A dry crawl space comes from a full ground barrier, an intact belly wrap, clear vents and good drainage.

### Should I seal the vents under my mobile home?

No. Sealing the vents traps humidity and any water that leaks under the home. If you want less moisture, put in a full ground vapor barrier, keep the vents clear and screened, and fix leaks and drainage problems instead.

### What is the best way to keep a mobile home crawl space dry?

Cover all of the ground with overlapped six mil polyethylene or better, repair the belly wrap, keep skirting vents open, send roof water away with gutters and downspouts, and fix plumbing and condensate leaks quickly. Check it each year after the rainy season.

### How much does a mobile home ground vapor barrier cost?

Our vapor barrier spot repairs run $400-$900, and full replacement runs $2,000-$4,000. Where a job falls depends on the size of the home, how much barrier is torn or missing, and how easy it is to get under the home. The written estimate is free.

### Do I still need vents if I have a vapor barrier?

Yes. HUD's standard lets the vent area drop from one square foot per 150 square feet of floor area to one per 1,500 when the ground is fully covered with a vapor retarder. It does not remove the need for ventilation openings.
