## Why Marion County Sits in Sinkhole Country

Marion County sits on limestone. Lots of it, and fairly close to the surface. Rainwater picks up a mild acid as it moves through soil, and over thousands of years that acid slowly dissolves the limestone underneath us. That process is called karst, and it's why Florida has springs, caverns, and yes, sinkholes.

The Florida Department of Environmental Protection keeps a whole program on this, and their maps show central Florida, including Marion County, in the zone where cover-subsidence and cover-collapse sinkholes both occur. You can read their plain-language overview at the [Florida Geological Survey sinkhole page](https://floridadep.gov/fgs/sinkholes).

Two types matter for homeowners:

**Cover-subsidence sinkholes** form slowly. Sand filters down into voids in the limestone over years, and the ground surface settles gradually. These are the common ones around here, and they usually show up as slow, spreading depressions rather than dramatic collapses.

**Cover-collapse sinkholes** are the ones that make the news. A clay layer bridges over a growing void until it suddenly can't, and the ground drops fast. These are rare, but they're the reason you shouldn't ignore certain warning signs.

Mobile homes actually give you an advantage here that site-built homes don't. Because your home sits on piers over a crawl space, the ground underneath is visible. You can literally look at the soil your home stands on, which means problems tend to announce themselves early if you know what to look for.

## Sinkhole Warning Signs Marion County Homeowners Notice First

Most sinkhole activity announces itself outside before it touches the home. Here's what to scan for on a monthly walk around your property.

**Circular or oval depressions in the yard.** Normal settling from fill dirt tends to be irregular. Sinkhole-related subsidence often forms a rough circle or bowl shape that deepens over weeks or months. If you can stand in the middle of a dip and see the ground sloping toward you from all sides, pay attention.

**Fence posts, trees, or skirting that start leaning toward one spot.** When soil moves down and inward toward a void, everything anchored in that soil tilts toward the center. One leaning fence post means a rotten post. Three posts leaning toward the same point means the ground is moving.

**A new pond that shouldn't be there.** Water finds low spots. If rain suddenly starts pooling where it never did before, or a wet spot stays soggy days after everything else dries out, the ground under it may have dropped. Standing water under the home is its own problem too, and we cover that in our post on [water under a mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/), and it deserves its own fix.

**Wilting vegetation in a defined patch.** When soil ravels downward, plant roots lose contact with moisture. A circle of stressed grass in an otherwise green yard is a classic early sign, according to the [USGS Water Science School](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/sinkholes).

**Cloudy well water.** If you're on a private well and your water suddenly turns cloudy or gritty, sediment may be moving underground. That's worth a call to a well contractor and worth noting if other signs appear.

None of these alone proves a sinkhole. All of them together, clustered in one area, is a pattern worth taking seriously.

## Sinkhole Signs Under a Mobile Home: What to Check in the Crawl Space

This is where mobile home owners have the edge. Twice a year, take a flashlight and look under your skirting. Here's what separates routine wear from a red flag.

**Piers that have sunk unevenly, fast.** Every mobile home in Florida settles some. Sandy soil compacts, and piers drop a quarter inch here and there over years. That's normal, and it's why releveling is routine maintenance. What's not normal is one section of piers dropping an inch or more in a few months while the rest stay put. Localized, rapid drop points to localized soil loss.

**A visible depression or soft cone in the crawl space soil.** Get low and shine your light across the ground under the home. The soil should be roughly flat. A bowl-shaped dip, especially one with loose sand funneling toward its center, is the single most direct sinkhole sign you can spot under a mobile home.

**Pier pads tilting toward a common point.** Same logic as the fence posts. Pads and footings tilt toward the soil that's leaving.

**Fresh gaps between the ground and pier pads.** If a pad that used to sit flush on the soil now has daylight under one edge, the ground moved. Note it, photograph it, and date the photo.

Inside the home, the signs echo what's happening below. Doors that suddenly won't latch, cracks that appear over doorways and windows, and floors that develop a slope all track ground movement. The catch is that every one of those symptoms is also a symptom of ordinary settling. ## Sinkhole or Settling? How to Tell the Difference

The honest answer is that in Marion County, settling explains the overwhelming majority of what homeowners see. Sandy soil, heavy summer rain, and piers doing their job under a moving load will produce sticking doors and hairline cracks all on their own. We wrote up the mechanics in [why mobile homes settle in Florida](/blog/why-mobile-homes-settle-florida/).

Still, there are patterns that point one way or the other. Use this table as a first filter, not a final verdict.

| What You See | Points to Normal Settling | Points to Possible Sinkhole Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of change | Gradual, over years | Fast, over weeks or a few months |
| Shape on the ground | Irregular, follows fill dirt or drainage | Circular or bowl-shaped depression |
| Location | Spread across the home, worst at edges | Concentrated in one zone, inside and out |
| Cracks | Hairline, at seams and corners | Widening cracks that grow week to week |
| Yard clues | None, or general low spots after rain | Leaning posts, dying patch of grass, new pond |
| After releveling | Home stays level for years | Same section drops again within months |

That last row matters most. A properly releveled mobile home on stable soil should hold its level for three to five years or more. If we relevel a section and it sinks again in six months, we tell the homeowner plainly that the soil needs professional evaluation, and we help them find a licensed geotechnical firm. Releveling over an active void just buries the evidence.

## What to Do the Day You Spot Something

Keep this simple sequence handy.

**Step 1: Photograph and date everything.** Cracks, dips, leaning posts, gaps under piers. Put a tape measure or a coin in the frame for scale. A dated photo series is the cheapest diagnostic tool there is, proving whether things are changing and how fast.

**Step 2: Mark crack ends with a pencil.** Write the date at the tip of each crack. If the crack grows past your mark in a few weeks, you've turned a guess into data.

**Step 3: Stay out of obvious depressions.** If the ground has visibly dropped, don't walk on it, don't drive on it, and keep kids and pets away. Rope it off. If a hole opens suddenly near or under the home, get everyone out and call Marion County emergency management. That's the one true drop-everything scenario, and it's rare.

**Step 4: Get an under-home inspection.** For anything short of an emergency, start with a professional crawl-space evaluation. This is the stop-DIY line. Crawling under a home with actively shifting piers is dangerous, and probing suspect soil yourself can collapse a void. Let someone with jacks, experience, and insurance make the call.

**Step 5: Escalate honestly.** If the inspection points to settling, fix it and recheck in six months. If it points to the ground, bring in a licensed geotechnical engineer and loop in your insurance carrier early.

Watch the yard, check the crawl space twice a year, photograph changes, and act on patterns instead of single scary moments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How common are sinkholes in Marion County?

Marion County sits in Florida's karst region, so subsidence reports happen every year, but confirmed damaging sinkholes under homes remain rare. The Florida Geological Survey tracks subsidence incident reports statewide, and most turn out to be ordinary settling, buried debris, or erosion. Check under your home twice a year and act on fast changes.

### Will my insurance cover sinkhole damage to a mobile home?

Florida law requires insurers to offer catastrophic ground cover collapse coverage, which pays when the ground abruptly collapses, the home is condemned, and the damage is visible. Full sinkhole loss coverage, which pays for less dramatic sinkhole damage, is an optional endorsement many owners skip. Read your specific policy, because the two coverages pay in very different situations. Document everything with dated photos from the first sign onward, since claims often hinge on timelines.

### Can releveling fix a home over a sinkhole?

No, and any contractor who says otherwise is selling you the wrong fix. Releveling restores a home over stable soil, and it's the right repair for the vast majority of sunken piers in our area. If the soil itself is raveling into a void, the ground needs stabilization by a geotechnical specialist first, usually through compaction grouting. After the ground is certified stable, releveling is the finishing step, not the cure.

### How do I tell sinkhole activity from normal settling after heavy rain?

Rain-related settling is broad, gradual, and worst where drainage is poor, and it usually stabilizes once the soil dries. Sinkhole activity is concentrated, often circular, and keeps progressing in dry weather. If the same spot keeps dropping after repairs, or a depression deepens without rain, treat it as a soil problem, not a leveling problem.
