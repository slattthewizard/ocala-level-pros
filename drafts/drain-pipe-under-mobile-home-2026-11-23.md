# Drain Pipes Under a Mobile Home: Sagging Lines, Leaks and Leveling

The drain pipes under a mobile home are plastic lines that run just below the floor and carry wastewater out to the sewer or septic connection. They are meant to slope steadily downhill and stay supported. When a line sags, cracks or pulls loose at a joint, wastewater leaks onto the ground and soaks the belly, and the first sign is often a smell or a damp patch you can see through the access panel.

We are not plumbers, and we don't repair drain lines. Our work is under the home, though, where drain leaks soak the ground barrier and the belly, and releveling moves the pipes. This post covers all three.

## What Runs Under Your Home

Under most manufactured homes you will find a few lines. Drain lines from the kitchen, bathrooms and laundry run to a main line. The main line runs to the sewer or septic hookup. Water supply lines and heat tape or insulation may also be under there, and in a double-wide, drain lines cross from one section to the other.

The drain pipes are usually black or white plastic, and they hang from the floor framing on straps or hangers. They are not buried. That makes them easy to inspect, and easy to damage when something shifts.

## What HUD's Installation Standards Say

The federal installation standard for the drainage system is [24 CFR 3285.604](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.604). It is written for the installer setting up a home, and a few lines are worth knowing.

On slope, it says drain lines must not slope less than one-quarter inch per foot, unless otherwise noted on the schematic diagram. It also says a slope of one-eighth inch per foot may be permitted when a clean-out is installed at the upper end of the run.

On support, it says that parts of the drainage system shipped loose, such as crossover connections between sections, must be installed and supported in accordance with 3280.608. That construction rule says piping must be installed without undue strains and stresses, with provision for expansion, contraction and structural settlement. Unless the pipe maker says otherwise, it also says horizontal plastic drainage piping must be supported at intervals not to exceed 4 feet.

On testing, it says the drainage system must be inspected and tested for leaks after completion at the site.

We are quoting a standard for a new installation. It does not set a repair procedure for an older home, and we won't pretend it does. What it tells you is that these lines are meant to have a steady slope and proper support, and that a belly full of sagging, ponding pipe is not how the home was designed.

## Why Drain Lines Sag

Drain lines are plastic, and plastic bends over time. Sagging usually comes from one of these:

- **A hanger or strap has failed.** Straps rust, break or pull out of old framing, and the pipe drops.
- **Weight and heat.** A full pipe is heavy, and Florida heat softens plastic and lets it creep.
- **The home moved.** If a pier settles, the floor drops with it, and the pipe tied to the floor can end up with a low spot.
- **Animals or past work.** Something knocked a line loose, or earlier repairs were never supported.

A sag creates a belly in the pipe where water sits instead of draining. Solids settle there, and the line slows down or clogs. Once a line is slow, you may notice gurgling or backups inside the home. That can be a plumbing problem, so call a plumber.

## Signs of a Leak Under the Home

A drain leak under a home is different from a supply leak. Wastewater stinks and stains, and it is not clean water. Here is what to look for:

- A sewer smell inside the home, especially near a bathroom, kitchen or utility room
- A smell around the skirting that gets worse after you run water
- Wet or dark ground under one section, when the rest is dry
- Water dripping or dampness on the underside of the belly
- Stained, sagging or torn belly material near a pipe
- Mold or mildew on the framing or insulation
- Flies, gnats or other insects collecting in one spot under the home
- Soft spots in the floor near a bathroom, kitchen or laundry

Do not guess from the outside. Put on gloves, take a flashlight and look under the access panel. If you see wet ground, a dripping pipe or a pipe you can push on and move, stop and call a plumber. ## What Leaks Do to the Ground Barrier and Belly

A leak is not only a plumbing bill. Wastewater that lands on the ground barrier runs to a low spot and sits there. The ground stays wet, humidity under the home goes up, and the vapor barrier can tear, shift or stay soaked.

The belly takes the next hit. Wet insulation loses its value and holds water against the floor. Over time that rots the belly material and can reach the floor framing. Our post on the [mobile home belly board repair](/blog/mobile-home-belly-board-repair/) covers what belly damage looks like, and [water under a mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/) covers why standing water is a problem.

Moisture under the home also feeds rust on the frame and anchors, and attracts termites and other pests. A small leak that runs for months does more damage than a pipe that bursts once and gets fixed the same day.

## How Releveling Affects the Plumbing

This is the part owners rarely hear about. When a crew lifts a home to relevel it, the floor and the frame move, and the pipes hanging from the floor move with them. Rigid plastic lines are fairly forgiving over a small lift, but they can be stressed if the lift is large or sudden, or if a pipe is already tight.

Where it matters most:

- **Joints and connections.** Glued or threaded joints can crack or pull apart if the line is strained.
- **The sewer or septic connection.** The main line leaves the home at one point, and that connection can bind if the home lifts or drops.
- **Crossovers.** In a double-wide, the line that crosses between sections can shear if the two sides are lifted by different amounts.
- **Supply and gas lines.** Water supply connections can be strained too. Never touch a gas line yourself.

A good crew lifts in small steps, watches the connections as they go, and checks the pipes before and after. Ask any crew you hire whether they check plumbing connections during a lift. If a line is already cracked or hanging by one strap, the crew should tell you before the lift, not after.

## Never Get Under a Home on a Jack

If you are checking pipes, a word of safety. Never get under a home that is supported only by a jack. Jacks slip, and a home weighs thousands of pounds. If a home has been lifted for any reason, keep out from underneath until it is back on its piers or properly blocked. You can look at the pipes from the access panel with the home sitting on its normal supports.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the pipes under a mobile home?

They are mostly plastic drain lines that run from the kitchen, bathrooms and laundry to a main line, plus water supply lines. The main drain connects to the sewer or septic hookup. The drain lines hang from the floor framing and are not buried.

### How do I know if the drain pipe under my mobile home is leaking?

Look for a sewer smell, wet or dark ground in one spot, drips on the belly, stained or sagging insulation, and mold near a pipe. Check from the access panel with a flashlight and gloves. If you see a leak, call a plumber, since we don't repair drain lines.

### Why is my mobile home drain line sagging?

Common causes are failed hangers or straps, heat softening the plastic, and a settled pier that dropped part of the floor. HUD's installation standards call for drain lines to slope at least one-quarter inch per foot unless the diagram says otherwise, so a sag that holds water is a problem to fix.

### Can releveling my mobile home damage the plumbing?

It can if the lift is big or sudden, or if a pipe is already tight or cracked. Joints, the sewer connection and crossover lines in a double-wide are the weak points. A careful crew lifts in small steps and checks connections, and you should have a plumber repair any line that is already damaged.
