# Mobile Home Jacks: Types, Safe Use and When to Call a Crew

Most mobile home jacks are hydraulic bottle jacks or screw jacks, and they all do one job: lift the main steel I-beam a small amount so you can adjust a pier. They are lifting tools, not support. Nobody should ever be under a home that a jack alone is holding up.

This guide covers the jack types you will run into, where on the frame the jack goes, how much you can lift in one spot before something breaks, and the point where a weekend project becomes a crew job. If you want the full leveling process, our post on [DIY mobile home leveling](/blog/diy-mobile-home-leveling/) walks through it. This one is about the jack.

## The Jack Types You Will See

Not every jack that fits under a mobile home belongs there. Here is what actually gets used.

**Hydraulic bottle jacks.** A bottle jack is the usual pick for a hydraulic jack on a mobile home. It is compact, it fits in the crawlspace, and it lifts a lot for its size. The tradeoff is a small, narrow base and a limited stroke, so it needs a solid, wide footing under it and often a stack of cribbing to reach the frame.

**Screw jacks.** These lift by turning a threaded shaft. They are slow, but they hold their height without hydraulic fluid, so they do not creep down the way a tired hydraulic seal can. Pros like them for fine adjustment at a single pier.

**Floor jacks and scissor jacks.** A garage floor jack needs room to roll and a hard, flat surface, and the car-trunk scissor jack is not made for a house. Skip both.

**Jack stands and adjustable steel piers.** These get confused with jacks all the time, so here is the difference. A jack lifts. A jack stand or an adjustable steel pier holds. A car-style jack stand is rated for a car corner and has a small saddle, and it is not a substitute for a pier under a home. An adjustable steel pier is a support, and it carries the load for years. You can read more about pier choices in our [mobile home pier types](/blog/mobile-home-pier-types/) post.

Whatever you use, check the rated capacity against what you are lifting. A single-wide or double-wide weighs far more than any car. If the jack's rating is close to what the load might be, get a bigger jack or a second one.

## Where the Jack Goes on the Frame

The only place to lift is the main steel frame, the I-beam that runs the length of the home under each section. That is the structure the piers are built to carry.

Set the jack so it pushes up on the bottom flange of the I-beam, close to the pier you are working on. Not out at the far end of a span. Not on a crossmember that looks sturdy. The beam is meant to take a vertical load right there.

Where you do not jack:

- **The floor or the underbelly.** The floor is plywood or particleboard on joists, and the belly is thin material meant to keep pests and moisture out. A jack through either one goes through it.
- **The outrigger edge.** Outriggers are the short steel arms that stick out sideways from the main beam to hold the floor. They are not built to take a point load at the tip, and they bend.
- **The pier cap itself.** The jack lifts the beam off the pier. It should not sit on top of the pier it is trying to adjust.

On the ground side, the jack needs a solid base. In Marion County that matters more than people expect. Dry sand is loose, and wet sand turns soft. A bottle jack's small base will sink into either one the moment the load comes on. Set it on a wide, flat footing, such as a plank or a pad that spreads the load, and check it is plumb before you pump.

## How Much You Can Lift at One Spot

Less than you think. HUD's installation standards treat a home as adequately leveled when there is no more than 1/4 inch difference between adjacent pier supports and the exterior doors and windows do not bind and can be properly operated ([24 CFR 3285.6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.6)). That is a small tolerance. A good leveling job is adjusting by fractions of an inch to a couple of inches, not cranking one corner up a foot.

The same installation rules put limits on how the gap under the beam gets filled. Shims are used in pairs and driven in tightly so they do not occupy more than one inch of vertical height. Anything bigger than that is filled with hardwood plates or concrete block ([24 CFR 3285.304](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.304)). Manufactured piers have a limit too: they must be selected so the adjustable risers do not extend more than 2 inches when finally positioned. If you need much more lift than that, the pier is the wrong height, and the answer is a taller pier, not a bigger jack. Our post on [mobile home leveling shims](/blog/mobile-home-leveling-shims/) covers what is allowed in that gap.

Lift in small steps: a few pumps, then stop and look. Check the doors and windows as you go, not after.

## Why Lifting One Spot Cracks Drywall

A mobile home is a long, stiff box held up at a row of points. When you jack one point up, the frame twists a little to follow, and the walls above it have to go along. Drywall and trim are not built to flex. They crack first.

It shows up as a diagonal crack off a door or window corner, a seam opening in the ceiling, or a door that suddenly rubs. Lifting too far at one corner makes it worse, because the neighboring piers carry less and the frame bridges the gap between them.

That is why level is judged between adjacent piers, and why pros work the whole home. They lift a little at one pier, then move to the next, and they bring up the low side gradually along its length. Jacking up one sagging corner by itself fixes that corner and stresses everything beside it. ## Cribbing and Why a Jack Is Never the Support

Cribbing means stacked blocks or timbers under the load, built up as the jack lifts so the weight always has a solid backup. As the home goes up, you add cribbing. If the jack slips, the home drops onto the cribbing instead of onto you.

Some basics for cribbing:

- Use solid blocks or timbers, not hollow blocks stacked on their sides, and not bricks.
- Stack them in a crisscross pattern on a firm, flat base.
- Keep the stack tight to the underside of the beam, with only a small gap.

The hard rule: **never put any part of your body under a home that is held up by a jack alone.** Jacks slip. Bottle jack seals leak. The ground under the base shifts. A home weighs many tons, and there is no second chance. If you need to reach under, the load goes on cribbing or a proper support first, and even then you stay out from under when you can.

## When a Jack Is Enough, and When It Is Not

A jack makes sense for a small lift at one pier, to swap a shim, adjust a cap, or take the weight off a pad, with cribbing in place. Call a crew when:

- You need to lift more than the shim limits above allow.
- More than one corner or a whole side is off.
- The ground under the piers is soft, washed out, or sunk. The footing needs fixing, not just the height.
- Doors and windows already bind or walls are cracking, which tells you the home has already moved and needs a full check.
- You cannot reach the beam without crawling a long way under the home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What kind of jack do I need for a mobile home?

A hydraulic bottle jack or a screw jack, rated well above the load you will lift. Both fit in a crawlspace and lift the main steel I-beam. A car scissor jack is too light for the job, and a floor jack needs more room and a hard surface than you usually have under a home.

### Where do I put the jack under a mobile home?

Under the bottom flange of the main steel I-beam, close to the pier you are adjusting, on a wide solid base. Never lift on the floor, the underbelly, the tip of an outrigger, or the pier cap you are trying to adjust.

### Can I use a bottle jack to level a mobile home?

You can use one to lift the beam a small amount while you adjust a pier or shim, with cribbing in place as a backup. It is not the support, and it is not a good tool for lifting a corner a long way. If you need more than a small lift, the pier height is the problem, and that is a crew job.

### How high can I jack up a mobile home at one point?

Only a little. HUD's installation standards call a home level when adjacent piers are within 1/4 inch of each other, so large lifts at one spot stress the frame and crack drywall. Lift in small steps and work the whole home, not one corner.

### Is it safe to go under a mobile home held up by a jack?

No. Never get under a home that a jack alone is holding. Jacks slip, seals leak, and soft ground shifts. Put the load on cribbing or a proper pier first, and call a crew if you are unsure.
