# Do You Need a Permit to Level a Mobile Home in Florida?

You get a quote for $600 to relevel your mobile home, and then your neighbor leans over the fence and says, "You know you need a permit for that, right?" Now you're not sure if this is a one-day fix or a three-week paperwork headache.

Here's the honest answer about needing a permit to level a mobile home in Florida: it depends on what "leveling" actually involves and which county you live in. Routine releveling, where a crew adjusts shims and jacks the home back to level on its existing piers, usually doesn't require a building permit in most Florida counties. But the moment the job grows into replacing piers, adding new supports, changing anchors, or touching the foundation system, permits often come into play.

That gray area is where homeowners get burned. So let's walk through what Florida law actually says, how Marion County handles it, what the paperwork costs, and when you can relax because your contractor is handling all of it anyway.

## The Short Answer on Leveling Permits in Florida

Florida doesn't have one statewide rule that says "all mobile home leveling requires a permit." Instead, two layers of rules apply.

First, the state regulates who can do the work. Under [Florida Statute 320.8249](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/320.8249), anyone who performs mobile home installation work for pay must be a licensed mobile home installer. Florida defines installation broadly, and it includes blocking, leveling, supporting, and anchoring the home. So even if no permit is required for your specific job, the person doing it should hold a state installer license or work under someone who does.

Second, your county or city decides when a permit is required. Building departments across Florida treat simple releveling differently than structural work. Most treat shim adjustments and minor jacking as maintenance. Most treat pier replacement, new anchor installation, and foundation changes as permitted work that gets inspected.

The practical takeaway: the question isn't really "do I need a permit to level my home," it's "how much work does my home actually need?" A quick relevel is one answer. ## What Counts as "Leveling" and What Counts as Structural Work

This distinction decides everything, so let's make it concrete.

**Routine releveling** means the crew goes under the home with a water level or laser level, checks every pier, and adjusts shims and wedges to bring the frame back to level. The piers stay where they are. The anchors stay where they are. Nothing new gets installed. In most Florida counties, this is maintenance, not construction, and no permit is pulled. If you want to see what that process looks like step by step, we broke it down in [how mobile home leveling works](/blog/how-mobile-home-leveling-works/).

**Structural setup work** means something in the support system changes. Examples:

- Replacing cracked or crushed piers
- Adding piers where the frame is sagging between supports
- Installing new tie-down anchors or straps
- Repairing or replacing the footings under piers
- Converting to a permanent foundation for FHA financing

That second list is where counties typically want a permit and an inspection, because the work affects how the home handles wind loads. Florida takes wind resistance seriously for good reason, and the inspection is there to confirm the new piers and anchors meet the state's installation standards under Rule 15C-1.

Here's the thing most homeowners don't realize: about a third of "leveling" jobs turn into the second category once someone actually crawls under the home. Settling is usually a symptom. The cause might be soil movement, water pooling under the home, or piers that were spaced too far apart from day one. You can read more about why that happens in our post on [why mobile homes settle in Florida](/blog/why-mobile-homes-settle-florida/).

## Why the Installer License Matters More Than the Permit

Homeowners fixate on permits, but the state installer license is the piece that protects you day to day.

Florida requires mobile home installers to pass an exam, carry insurance, and renew their license through the state. The program exists because a mobile home's safety in high wind depends almost entirely on how it's blocked, leveled, and anchored. You can verify licensing requirements and look up installer information through the [Florida Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles](https://www.flhsmv.gov/) site, which oversees mobile home installation standards in the state.

Why does this matter for a "simple" leveling job? Because leveling is never just lifting. A licensed installer knows how much to jack at each point so you don't crack drywall or twist the frame, how to spot a pier that will fail in a year, and when straps and anchors no longer meet spec. An unlicensed handyman with a bottle jack knows none of that, and the damage he causes usually shows up months later as new wall cracks and doors that won't latch. We've written about those warning signs in [cracks in mobile home walls](/blog/cracks-mobile-home-walls/).

This is also where DIY has a hard stop. Checking your own floors with a level? Great idea. Crawling under a 15,000 pound structure to jack it up yourself? Please don't. Homes shift off jacks, and people get killed that way. There's no permit for a DIY relevel because the state assumes you won't be doing one.

So when you're comparing quotes, ask two questions before you ask about price: are you a licensed Florida mobile home installer, and do you pull permits when the job needs them? ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a permit just to relevel my mobile home on its existing piers?

In most Florida counties, no. Adjusting shims and releveling on existing supports is generally treated as maintenance rather than construction. The rules change if piers are added or replaced, or if anchors and straps are installed, so the real answer depends on what the inspection finds under your home.

### Who pulls the permit when one is required, me or the contractor?

The contractor should. A licensed mobile home installer handles the application, fees, and inspection scheduling as part of the job. If a company asks you to pull an owner's permit for their work, treat that as a warning sign, because it can shift liability for their work onto you.

### Can I level my mobile home myself without a permit?

Florida's licensing rules apply to people doing installation work for pay, so the state isn't going to fine you for adjusting your own home. But jacking up a mobile home is genuinely dangerous, and a bad DIY lift can crack walls, twist the frame, and create bigger repair bills. Check your floors with a level all you want, then let a licensed installer do the lifting.

### Will unpermitted leveling work hurt my insurance or a future sale?

It can. Four point inspectors and buyers' inspectors look at the support and tie-down system, and undocumented or obviously improvised work raises flags that stall closings and coverage. Permitted, documented repairs do the opposite, giving you paperwork that answers questions before they're asked.
