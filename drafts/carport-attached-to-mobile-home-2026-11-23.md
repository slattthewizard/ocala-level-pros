# Carport, Porch or Deck Attached to a Mobile Home: What to Know

You can attach a carport, porch or deck to a mobile home, but it should carry its own weight. HUD's installation standards say an attached structure is designed to support all of its own live and dead loads, so the home's frame, walls and roof should not be holding it up. When they are, the home pays for it.

We don't build carports, porches, decks or Florida rooms. We do relevel homes, and an addition tied into the frame changes how a home settles and how it can be leveled. This post covers what the federal text says, what goes wrong, and what to ask your builder before anyone drills into your walls.

## What HUD's Installation Standards Say

The section that matters is [24 CFR 3285.903](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.903), titled "Permits, alterations, and on-site structures." Two parts of it are worth knowing.

On permits, it says that prior to making any alteration to a home or its installation, you should contact the local authority having jurisdiction to find out whether plan approval and permits are required.

On attached structures, it says each attached accessory building or structure or add-on "is designed to support all of its own live and dead loads," unless it is otherwise included in the installation instructions or designed by a registered professional engineer or registered architect.

A few things to keep straight. The section is written as a recommendation for what installation instructions should include, so it is not a full building code for your carport. It does not tell you how to flash a roof or how far a footing has to go. And it says nothing about your particular home, which is why the builder needs to look at the data plate and the manufacturer's documents. Our post on the [HUD data plate](/blog/mobile-home-hud-data-plate/) explains where to find yours.

## Permits in Marion County

Plan on a building permit. Section 3285.903 itself tells you to contact the local authority before altering a home, and in Marion County that means the county's building office, or the city if you live inside city limits. If your home sits in a park, you have a second set of rules. Parks often limit what you can attach and how it looks, and some require written approval first. ## Free-Standing or Attached

The cleanest carport is one that does not touch the home at all. It has its own posts and footings, and sits a few inches off the wall. Nothing it does can reach the home.

An attached structure can work too, if it is built to carry itself. The posts go on their own footings, and the connection to the home just keeps the two from drifting apart. The addition and the home are two separate structures that happen to sit next to each other.

The version that causes trouble is the one that borrows from the home. A ledger bolted to the wall to carry a roof, or a porch frame screwed to the rim of the floor, puts the addition's weight on a structure that was designed to carry its own.

## Why Settling Makes It Worse

Mobile homes sit on piers over sandy soil. In Marion County, heavy summer rain softens the ground and the soil under a pier can compress or wash. When a pier settles, the home sinks a little at that point.

Now picture a porch or carport tied into the home. Its posts sit on footings that may not settle at the same rate. Two things can happen:

- **The addition holds the home up.** A rigid porch frame ties into a corner and props it while the rest of the home settles. The corner stays high, and you end up with a home that is out of level in a way that looks strange.
- **The home pulls the addition down.** If the corner under the addition settles, the home drags the porch or carport with it. Posts lean, the roof pulls away from the wall, and the connection is under stress it was never meant to take.

In both cases the symptoms show up inside the home: doors that stick, gaps at trim, cracks near the attachment. If you see those, our guide to [doors that won't close in a mobile home](/blog/mobile-home-doors-wont-close/) is a good place to start.

## Flashing, Leaks and Water Damage

The connection between a roof and a wall is where leaks often start. A carport roof or porch roof that meets the home's siding needs flashing that sheds water outward. Done poorly, rain runs behind the siding and into the wall, and you may not see it for a year.

Florida's heat and storms are hard on that joint. Sealant dries out, and the home and the addition move a little with every season. Check these spots once a year:

- Stains on the ceiling or wall inside near the attachment
- Soft or swollen siding along the connection
- Gaps or cracked caulk where the roof meets the wall
- Water pooling next to the home where the addition drains

A leak into the wall is a roofing and carpentry problem, so call that trade. But keep in mind that water that gets into the floor system can reach the belly and the ground below. Our post on [water under a mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/) covers what that does.

## Blocked Vents, Skirting and Access

An addition built tight to the home often shuts off parts of it you still need. Check these before you build:

- **Crawlspace vents.** Skirting vents need airflow to keep the underside from staying wet. A deck or porch built over them blocks that. See our post on [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/).
- **The skirting itself.** If posts or framing land where the skirt has to run, the skirt gets cut around them, and gaps let animals and water in.
- **The access panel.** You need to get under the home. Do not build over it.
- **Anchors and tie-downs.** Straps run from the frame to ground anchors. A new post or footing in the wrong spot can interfere with them.
- **Utilities.** Meters, shutoffs and the drain line may be right where you want to build.

None of this is hard to fix on paper. It just has to be thought about before the concrete is poured.

## Questions to Ask Before You Build

Take these to any contractor before you sign:

1. Will the addition stand on its own footings, or will it carry any load from the home?
2. Who pulls the permit, and has the county signed off on the plans?
3. How will the roof connect to the home, and who is responsible if it leaks?
4. Does the design allow for the home to settle or be releveled later?
5. What happens to my skirting, vents and access panel?
6. Will you stay clear of the tie-down straps and anchors?
7. If my park or HOA has rules, did you check them?

A contractor who has built on manufactured homes before will answer all of these without hesitation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you attach a carport to a mobile home?

Yes, but the carport should support its own weight. HUD's installation standards say an attached structure is designed to carry all of its own live and dead loads unless it is covered by the installation instructions or an engineer's or architect's design. Many people avoid the issue by building the carport free-standing, a few inches off the home.

### Do I need a permit to add a porch or deck to my mobile home?

Plan on it. HUD's standards advise contacting the local authority before making any alteration to a home, and Marion County's building office can tell you what it requires. Park rules may add their own approval step, so ask the park too.

### Can a porch or carport make my mobile home unlevel?

It can. If the addition is tied into the home, it can hold one corner up while the rest settles, or the home can be pulled by an addition that settles on its own. Doors that stick and cracks near the connection are common signs. A leveling inspection can show whether the attachment is part of the problem.
