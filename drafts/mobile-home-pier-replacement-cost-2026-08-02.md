## What Mobile Home Pier Replacement Cost Looks Like Per Pier

Here's the honest range we see across Ocala and Marion County, plus Sumter and Citrus counties:

- **Single pier replacement (standard block):** $150 to $400
- **Pier with new footing pad:** $250 to $500
- **Steel or adjustable pier upgrade:** $300 to $600
- **Re-stacking a tipped pier (no new materials):** $75 to $200
- **Shim and cap adjustment only:** $40 to $100 per pier as part of a leveling job

Why the wide range? A pier isn't just a stack of blocks. The price includes crawling under the home, jacking the frame safely, removing the failed pier, setting a new footing on stable soil, stacking and capping the new pier, and lowering the home back onto it. Labor and access are most of the bill, not the concrete.

That's also why replacing five piers doesn't cost five times as much as replacing one. Once a crew is under the home with jacks set, each additional pier gets cheaper. Expect a minimum service charge of around $300 to $500 for any single-pier visit, because the setup work is the same whether we replace one pier or eight.

## Why Piers Fail in Central Florida

Florida is hard on piers, and Marion County soil is a big part of it. Most of our area sits on sandy soil over limestone, and sand moves when it gets wet. Our summer rain pattern means the ground under your home swells and shrinks a little every year.

Here's what actually kills piers around here:

**Settling soil.** The number one cause. The footing pad sinks unevenly, the pier tips, and the weight shifts to neighboring piers. That's why one bad pier often becomes three.

**Washout.** Poor drainage or a skirting gap lets rainwater channel under the home. Water carries sand away from under the footing, and the pier drops. Homes near retention areas and low lots in Summerfield and Lady Lake see this a lot.

**Cracked blocks.** Concrete blocks handle compression well, but once a home goes out of level, blocks start carrying weight at an angle. That's when they crack and crumble.

**Bad original installation.** We still find piers set on bare sand with no pad, or stacked with the block cores facing sideways. Pre-1976 homes are the usual suspects, but we've seen it on homes from the 2000s too.

The good news: pier failure is slow and it announces itself. Doors that won't latch, cracks at window corners, a bounce in the hallway floor. If you're seeing those signs, pay attention, because pier problems and leveling problems are usually the same problem.

## What Drives Your Pier Replacement Cost Up or Down

Two neighbors can call us the same week and get quotes that differ by $1,000. Here's why.

**Number of piers.** The biggest factor, obviously. A single wide typically sits on 12 to 20 piers, a double wide on 24 to 40 plus centerline supports. Replacing 3 piers is a half-day job. Replacing 15 is a full releveling project.

**Access under the home.** A home with 30 inches of clearance and removable skirting panels is fast. A home sitting 14 inches off the grade with mud underneath takes twice as long, and time is money.

**Soil condition.** If the old pier failed because of washout or soft soil, we can't just set a new pier in the same hole. Larger footings, ABS pads, or compacted fill add $50 to $150 per pier but stop you from paying for this again in five years.

**Whether leveling is included.** Here's something a lot of homeowners miss: you almost never replace piers without releveling, because the home has already shifted by the time a pier fails. ## Can You Replace a Mobile Home Pier Yourself?

Short answer: you can re-shim, but you shouldn't replace.

Adding or adjusting shims on an existing pier is a reasonable DIY job for a careful homeowner with the right gear. Replacing a pier is not, and here's why. To remove a pier, you have to jack the steel frame and hold thousands of pounds of home on temporary supports. If a jack kicks out or the frame slips, there is no second chance under there. Crews use multiple jacks, cribbing, and a water level or laser to keep the frame within tolerance while the weight is off.

There's also the accuracy problem. Set the new pier an eighth of an inch too high and you've just transferred stress to the piers on either side. You won't feel it for months, and then you'll feel it in your floors and door frames.

The DIY math rarely works anyway. By the time you rent jacks, buy blocks, pads, caps, and shims, you've spent $150 to $250 to do one pier, without insurance, without a warranty, and without anyone checking the other 25 piers. Worker safety agencies treat work under supported loads as a serious hazard for good reason; OSHA's guidance on jacking and cribbing safety at [osha.gov](https://www.osha.gov/) applies to pros, and the risks don't shrink for homeowners.

## How to Keep Pier Costs Down Long Term

The cheapest pier is the one you never have to replace. A few habits make a real difference in Florida:

**Fix drainage first.** Gutters, downspout extensions, and grading that moves water away from the skirting will do more for your piers than any upgrade. Most washout failures we see were preventable with $200 of drainage work.

**Get under the home once a year.** Or have someone do it. A 20-minute look catches a tipping pier while it's still a $100 re-stack instead of a $400 replacement plus subfloor damage.

**Recheck level every 3 to 5 years.** Florida homes settle. A periodic check with minor shim adjustments keeps weight distributed evenly so no single pier gets overloaded.

**Keep skirting sealed but vented.** Skirting keeps rushing water and animals out, while vents let moisture escape so your piers, straps, and belly board aren't sitting in a swamp.

**Bundle work.** If you need piers, tie-downs, and a vapor barrier, doing them in one visit saves you two extra trip charges and gets everything documented at once for your insurer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to replace all the piers under a mobile home?

Full pier replacement on a single wide typically runs $3,000 to $8,000, and a double wide can run $6,000 to $15,000 depending on pier count and access. That said, full replacement is rare. Most homes need a handful of piers replaced and the rest adjusted, which usually lands between $1,000 and $3,000 including releveling.

### How do I know if a pier needs replacement or just adjustment?

Cracked, crumbling, or shifted blocks mean replacement, and so does a footing that has sunk into the soil. A pier that has tipped but still has sound blocks can usually be re-stacked and shimmed for much less. A contractor should show you photos or walk you under the home so you can see the difference yourself before you approve anything.

### Does homeowners insurance cover mobile home pier replacement?

Usually not for normal settling, since insurers treat that as maintenance. Coverage sometimes applies when a covered event like a storm or a plumbing leak caused the damage, so it's worth a call to your agent with photos in hand. Keep written documentation of any pier work, because it helps at four-point inspection time and when you sell.

### How long does pier replacement take?

Replacing one to four piers is typically a half-day job, and most homes are done in a single day even with releveling included. Larger jobs with poor access or many footings can take two days. You can stay home during the work, though you'll hear some creaks as the home comes back to level.
