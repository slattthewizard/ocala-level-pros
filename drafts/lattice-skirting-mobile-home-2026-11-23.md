# Lattice Skirting for a Mobile Home: Pros, Cons and Florida Problems

Lattice skirting on a mobile home is cheap, easy to find, and lets the crawlspace breathe. It also lets raccoons, armadillos and rats walk right in unless it is backed with mesh, and wood lattice near the soil rots and gives termites a path in. Vinyl lattice fixes the rot, but not the open holes.

We do not install lattice. Our crew installs vinyl and rigid-foam skirting only. We do see a lot of lattice from underneath, though, so this post covers what holds up, what fails, and what to check if that is what surrounds your home.

## Why People Pick Lattice

The appeal is simple. A sheet of lattice costs less than most skirting panels, it cuts with basic tools, and it looks like a porch skirt, which some homeowners like. Because it is open, air moves through it on every side, so the space under the home dries out faster than it does behind solid panels with a few vents.

That airflow is the main real advantage. A lot of Marion County crawlspaces stay damp from sandy soil, summer downpours and a roofline that dumps water at the walls, so a skirt that never traps humidity has some value. The trade is that nearly everything else the skirt is supposed to do suffers.

## Wood Lattice Skirting

Wood lattice is usually thin pine or cedar strips stapled into a diamond pattern and set in a wood frame. It is the cheapest version and the first to go.

Here is what happens to it in Florida:

- **Rot at the bottom edge.** The bottom of the frame sits at or below grade and soaks up every rain. Thin strips can go soft in a couple of wet summers.
- **Termite contact.** Wood touching soil is an open door for subterranean termites. Lattice that runs into the dirt hands them a path toward the home's framing and trim.
- **Warping and splitting.** Heat and humidity twist untreated strips, and the lattice pulls out of its frame.
- **Constant repainting or staining.** Unsealed wood grays and cracks fast in full sun.

HUD's installation standards address wood skirting directly. Wood skirting within 6 inches of the ground must be pressure-treated for ground contact or be naturally resistant to decay and termite infestations ([24 CFR 3285.504(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.504)). Plain home-center lattice usually is not rated for that. If you keep wood, the lattice and the frame both need to be treated for ground contact, and the bottom edge is better held a few inches above the soil.

## Vinyl Lattice Skirting

Vinyl lattice looks like wood lattice, but it will not rot or feed termites, and it needs no paint. It comes in sheets or panels that screw into a frame or fit into a track.

It has its own problems:

- **Thin and flexible.** A sheet can bow, crack at the corners, or pop out of the frame in wind.
- **Sun damage.** Cheap vinyl fades and goes brittle on the sunny side of the home.
- **Still open.** The rot is gone, but the holes are the same size, so animals still get through.

Vinyl lattice is the better pick over wood in this climate. It is not a substitute for a solid skirt if you have an animal problem.

## The Animal Problem

This is the biggest drawback. Lattice holes are usually big enough for a cat, a possum or a rat, and a raccoon or armadillo can push a thin strip aside in one night. Once something is under the home, it can tear up the belly board and insulation, chew wire and pipe wrap, and leave droppings and a smell.

The fix is to back the lattice with galvanized hardware cloth or similar metal mesh on the inside face. Staple or screw the mesh to the frame so there are no gaps at the corners, and fasten or bury the bottom edge so nothing can dig under. Plastic window screen does not count. ## Ventilation: A Good Thing, With a Catch

HUD's rule for skirted crawlspaces calls for ventilation openings, and says the openings must be covered with a perforated corrosion and weather-resistant covering designed to prevent the entry of rodents ([24 CFR 3285.505(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505)). Read that last part again. Open lattice with no backing is airflow without the rodent-proof covering. Mesh is not an optional extra on lattice. It is what makes the openings do their job.

Open lattice also costs you some of what skirting is for. Wind-driven rain blows straight through, and the ground under the home gets wet on the windward side. Heat and cold move through freely too. If you are trying to keep the belly dry, a solid skirt with proper vents does that better. ## Framing and Installation Basics

Lattice only works as well as the frame holding it. If you or your park are putting it up, these points decide whether it lasts:

1. **Use treated lumber for the frame.** Top and bottom rails and any posts need to be rated for ground contact if they get near the soil.
2. **Keep the frame off the dirt.** A bottom rail resting on a concrete block, a paver or gravel stays drier than one buried in sand.
3. **Fasten into solid framing, not just the siding.** Do not attach it in a way that traps water between the siding and trim, and do not block the home's siding from expanding and contracting. HUD's skirting section covers both.
4. **Plan the access panel.** You need a removable section at least 18 inches wide and 24 inches tall so someone can reach the utility connections under the home. HUD sets that minimum in [24 CFR 3285.505(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505).
5. **Mesh before lattice.** Attach the hardware cloth to the frame first, then fasten the lattice over it.
6. **Leave room at the top.** Keep a small gap where the lattice meets the home so water does not sit behind the trim.

Do not cut the bottom edge to follow a sloped yard unless you add a rail or a mesh apron below the cut. A gap of an inch or two at the bottom is all a raccoon needs.

## Park Rules and HOA Limits

Many mobile home parks and communities set their own skirting rules. Some do not allow wood, some require a specific color or material, and some do not allow lattice on the street side at all. Check your lease or park rules and ask management before you buy materials. A skirt you have to tear off and redo costs you twice.

Lattice around a porch or carport is a different matter, since that is a separate structure from the home's skirting. We do not build porches, decks or carports, so ask a carpenter about those.

## Lattice vs Solid Skirting

Side by side:

| | Wood lattice | Vinyl lattice | Solid vinyl or foam skirt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rot and termite risk | High | None | None |
| Keeps animals out | Not without mesh | Not without mesh | Yes, with vent screens |
| Airflow | Very high | Very high | Moderate, set by vents |
| Blocks wind-driven rain | Poor | Poor | Good |
| Upkeep | Paint, repair, replace | Low | Low |

If your main goals are ventilation and a low price, lattice with mesh can work. If you want a dry belly, a tidier look and fewer repairs, a solid skirt wins. Our post on [mobile home skirting options](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-options/) compares the main materials and what each is good at.

## What We Check When We See Lattice

We do not replace lattice, but when we are under a home that has it, a few things matter to the work we do:

- Whether the lattice or its frame touches soil, which means rot and termite risk for the skirt and the home's framing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is lattice good for mobile home skirting?

It is fine for airflow and cost, but it has real drawbacks. Open lattice does not keep animals out unless it is backed with metal mesh, wood lattice rots and attracts termites where it touches soil, and the openings let wind-driven rain reach the ground under the home. Vinyl lattice with mesh behind it is the better version.

### Does lattice skirting keep animals out?

Not by itself. The holes are big enough for rats, cats and possums, and a raccoon or armadillo can push thin strips aside. Backing the lattice with galvanized hardware cloth, fastened with no gaps and secured at the bottom, is what makes it hold.

### Is wood lattice or vinyl lattice better for a mobile home?

Vinyl lattice is the better choice in Florida because it does not rot, does not feed termites and needs no paint. Wood lattice can work if both the lattice and the frame are pressure-treated for ground contact and the bottom edge is kept off the soil, but it takes more upkeep.

### Can I put lattice on a mobile home without a frame?

No. Lattice sheets are thin and flex in the wind, so they need a frame along the top and bottom, plus posts or backing where sheets meet. Without one they bow, pull loose and leave gaps at the corners. The frame also needs to be fastened into the home's solid framing and kept off the dirt.
