# How to Install Mobile Home Skirting (and the Steps DIY Kits Leave Out)

If you're researching how to install mobile home skirting, you've probably already opened the instructions folded inside a vinyl panel kit. Those instructions aren't wrong, exactly. They just assume the home underneath is already level, the ground is already graded, and nobody has to think much about vents or an access door. Plenty of homes in Marion County don't fit that picture.

This guide covers what the kit skips: what has to happen before the first panel goes up, how the install works step by step, the federal rules it has to follow, and the Florida problems that wreck jobs that looked fine on installation day.

Skirting is trim, not structure. It doesn't fix a home that's out of level, and it shouldn't go on until the home underneath it is right. If you want a second opinion first, [request a free written estimate](/contact/) and we'll tell you what we see.

## Before Any Skirting Goes On, the Home Has to Be Right

Skirting is the last thing that goes on a mobile home, not the first. Install it before the home is level, before the piers and anchors are set, and before a vapor barrier is down, and you're building a nice looking wall around problems about to get worse.

A home that settles after skirting is up pulls panels out of their track on one side and crushes them on the other. A home that needs a relevel means pulling skirting back off. A wet or bare crawl space under sealed skirting just traps the moisture in.

Before you buy a single panel, walk the perimeter and check the usual warning signs: doors that stick, floors that slope, gaps opening at one corner but not another. Our post on [signs a mobile home needs leveling](/blog/signs-mobile-home-needs-leveling/) covers what to look for.

The same goes for the ground underneath. Bare dirt or torn plastic instead of an intact vapor barrier is worth fixing while the crawl space is still open. A [vapor barrier](/vapor-barrier-ocala/) installed before skirting is much easier than one installed after.

Level and anchor the home, deal with the ground and moisture, then skirt it. Do it out of order and you'll likely end up redoing some of the work.

## What You're Installing: Vinyl, Metal, or Rigid Foam

Vinyl is the most common choice: interlocking panels that snap into a top rail and sit in a ground track, often made to look like a low brick or stone wall from a few feet away. It's the material this guide focuses on.

Metal skirting (usually aluminum panels) and rigid foam insulated panels are the other two options. The install sequence is close to the same for all three: measure, set a track, cut panels to fit, handle corners, vents, and the access door, then grade. The differences: fastening (metal is typically screwed rather than snapped), room for expansion (vinyl moves more in the heat), and cutting (rigid foam cuts cleanly with a fine-tooth blade, vinyl scores and snaps, metal needs snips or a shear).

If you haven't picked a material, our guide to [mobile home skirting options](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-options/) breaks down vinyl, metal, brick, and concrete side by side. Once you've picked, the rest of this guide walks through putting it on.

## Measuring, Framing, and Setting the Track

Start by measuring the full perimeter, not just one side. Most lots in Marion County aren't perfectly flat, so the height from the bottom of the siding to the ground changes as you go around, sometimes by several inches corner to corner. Measure that height every few feet and write the numbers down. That variation determines how tall your panels get cut at each point, and it's the thing DIY kit instructions assume away.

The top rail (also called the J-channel or starter rail) is what the panels hang from, fastened along the bottom edge of the home. This is an easy place to go wrong. Mount it so it forms a lip that catches rainwater, and it holds that water against the trim instead of letting it drip clear. Follow the maker's instructions for how the rail meets the siding, fasten through the slots so it can move, and don't caulk the top edge shut. Water needs a way out, not a place to collect.

At the bottom, the ground track needs to sit on ground graded firm and reasonably level, not loose fill that'll wash out after the first storm. If the ground slopes toward the home anywhere, fix that grading before the track goes down.

Leave room for the home and panels to move. Vinyl expands and contracts a fair amount between a cool morning and a July afternoon in direct sun, so fasteners get set snug, not cranked tight, and panels aren't cut to a jam fit against corners or the track. The home keeps settling in small amounts too, even after a relevel. Zero slack anywhere means the job buckles, cracks, or pops out of its track the first time either one happens.

## Cutting Panels, Corners, Vents, and the Access Door

With the top rail and ground track set, panels go in next, cut to the height measured at that spot and snapped or fastened into place one after another. Corners take a little more care: most systems use a corner post or trim piece rather than mitering the panel itself, and getting that piece plumb before you build off it saves you from a crooked run down the whole wall.

Vents and the access door get cut into the panel run, not treated as an afterthought. Plan their locations before you start cutting. The next section covers exactly how many vents and how big the access door needs to be, since those numbers come from federal code, not preference.

The other cuts are for whatever passes through the skirting: the dryer vent, any AC line set, and any plumbing that exits below the siding line. Each gets its own clean cutout with enough clearance that the panel isn't pinching a line, and enough that a future repair doesn't mean cutting it again. A dryer vent needs to terminate outside the skirting, not inside the crawl space, or it fills that space with lint and moist air.

Last step is backfilling and grading. Grade the soil along the base so water runs away from the home, not toward it. A steady slope away from the skirting for the first few feet handles most of it. Skip this and the first hard summer thunderstorm pools water right against the base of the panels, exactly the spot skirting is supposed to keep dry.

## The Code Rules Behind a Proper Skirting Job

Skirting isn't structural, but federal manufactured housing installation standards still set rules for it. Know these before you install it or hire the work out.

Under [24 CFR § 3285.504](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.504), skirting has to be weather-resistant, or protected at least as well as zinc-coated steel. It can't trap water between the siding and trim or force it up into the wall cavity, the exact mistake flagged above with the top rail, and it can't be fastened so tight it stops the siding from expanding and contracting. Any wood within 6 inches of the ground has to be pressure-treated for ground contact or a naturally decay- and termite-resistant species. Untreated lumber that close to Marion County dirt won't last.

Ventilation gets its own short paragraph because the numbers matter. Federal rule, [24 CFR § 3285.505](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505), requires vent openings on at least two opposite sides of the crawl space, screened with a rodent-proof covering, plus at least one access opening measuring 18 by 24 inches and at least 3 square feet in area. Vent area needed scales with your home's floor area, laid out separately in [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/).

The dryer vent also has to pass through the skirting to the outside, tying back to the cutout work above. None of this is optional trim-out preference. It's what keeps moisture from building up in a closed crawl space, and it's what a careful buyer's inspector looks at before a sale.

## Florida Problems Skirting Has to Survive

Getting the install right on day one is half the job. Marion County weather and wildlife test it for years afterward.

Summer storms and hurricane-strength wind are the biggest threat to panels staying put. Wind gets under a loose panel edge and pulls it straight out of the track, especially on a wall facing open ground. Fastening to the manufacturer's spacing instead of skipping screws, and checking the skirting after any significant storm, catches loose panels before a small problem becomes a missing one. Our [hurricane prep checklist for mobile homes](/blog/hurricane-prep-mobile-home-checklist/) covers what else to walk before and after a storm.

Termites are a year-round concern here, and skirting against mulch or dense landscaping gives them a bridge to the home. Keep mulch pulled back a few inches so there's a visible gap to check.

Gaps are the other recurring problem, whether from a popped panel, a track pulled from uneven ground, or a corner never sealed tight. Animals treat any gap as an open door, and once under a home they go after insulation and wiring. Our post on [keeping animals out from under a mobile home](/blog/animals-under-mobile-home/) covers how to find and close those gaps.

One more thing to check before you start: many manufactured home parks set their own rules on skirting material, color, or style, separate from federal code. Check with management before you order panels. [Park repair responsibility in Florida](/blog/mobile-home-park-repair-responsibility/) covers where those rules typically show up and who's on the hook for what.

## DIY or Hire It Out

A handy homeowner with a helper can install vinyl skirting on a small, level single-wide. Where DIY jobs go wrong is almost never the cutting and snapping together of panels. It's the parts that are easy to skip:

- **Installing over a home that isn't actually level.** A corner settles a half inch and that section buckles while the rest of the wall still looks fine.
- **Guessing at gaps instead of measuring for movement.** A jam-fit install is the first thing to crack or pop loose in the summer heat.
- **Getting the vent math wrong.** Not enough vent area, or vents on only one side, traps moisture instead of clearing it.
- **Skipping or undersizing the access door.** It's tempting to shrink it to save a panel, and then nobody can reach the plumbing.
- **Trapping water at the top rail.** One of the easiest details to get backward.

Those mistakes don't show up on installation day. They show up later, as buckled panels, a musty crawl space, or a repair bill bigger than doing it right the first time. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I install mobile home skirting myself?

Yes, on a home that's already level and sitting on graded ground, a handy homeowner can install vinyl skirting. The panels aren't difficult to cut and snap together. Where DIY jobs usually go wrong is skipping the parts that don't show up until later: correct vent counts, room for panels to move, and a top rail that doesn't trap water.

### Do I need to level my mobile home before installing skirting?

Yes. Skirting installed over a home that's out of level will bind, buckle, or pull out of its track once the home settles or gets releveled, and you'll end up redoing sections of it. Leveling, pier work, and anchoring should happen before the first panel goes up, not after.

### How many vents and access doors does skirting need?

Federal rule requires ventilation openings on at least two opposite sides of the crawl space, screened against rodents, plus at least one access opening measuring 18 by 24 inches and at least 3 square feet in area. The total vent area needed scales with the size of your home's floor.

### Should skirting touch the ground?

The bottom rail or ground track should sit on ground graded firm and level, close enough that gaps and daylight don't show underneath, but it isn't buried or sealed into the dirt. It needs to stay clear enough for water to drain away and for the vents to keep working.
