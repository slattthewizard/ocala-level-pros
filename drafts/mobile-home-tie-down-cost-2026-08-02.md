# Mobile Home Tie-Down Installation Cost in Florida

A full set of tie-downs on a double wide in Marion County usually runs $2,000 to $3,500. That single number often decides whether a homeowner passes a 4-point inspection and keeps their insurance, or starts scrambling for a new carrier three weeks before renewal.

If you're pricing this out, here's the honest picture. Mobile home tie down cost in Florida breaks down to roughly $50 to $85 per anchor installed, and most homes need somewhere between 10 and 30 anchors depending on size, age, and wind zone. Straps, stabilizer plates, and labor are usually rolled into that per-anchor price, but not always, which is why two quotes for the same house can be $1,200 apart.

This guide walks through the real numbers we see in Ocala, Belleview, Dunnellon, and the rest of our service area. You'll see what drives the price up, what a fair quote looks like, and where cutting corners actually costs you more.

## Average Mobile Home Tie-Down Cost in Florida

Let's start with the totals, because that's what your budget cares about.

For a **single wide**, a complete tie-down installation typically lands between **$1,200 and $2,500**. Single wides in Wind Zone 2, which covers all of Marion County, generally need frame anchors spaced no more than 5 feet 4 inches apart along both main rails, plus longitudinal anchors at the ends.

For a **double wide**, expect **$2,000 to $3,500** for a full system. Double wides need more anchors, and the marriage line and centerline supports add complexity.

Partial work costs less. If an inspector flagged a handful of loose straps or a few rusted anchor heads, a repair visit might run $400 to $900. Replacing every anchor on an older home where the originals have rusted through is closer to a full install price, because pulling bad anchors and setting new ones takes about the same labor as starting fresh.

## What Drives Tie-Down Installation Cost Up or Down

Two identical homes two streets apart can get different quotes for good reasons. Here are the big levers.

**Anchor count.** This is the largest factor by far. A 1985 single wide that was anchored to old standards might need 8 new anchors added to reach current spacing. A home that was never properly anchored needs the full set. Florida's installation standards in Rule 15C-1 of the Florida Administrative Code set the spacing, and the state's mobile home installer program is overseen by the [Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles](https://www.flhsmv.gov/motor-vehicles-tags-titles/mobile-homes/).

**Soil conditions.** Auger anchors screw into the ground, and Marion County soil ranges from cooperative sandy loam to limestone that stops an anchor cold. Rocky ground can mean longer anchors, different anchor types, or extra labor. Sugar sand at some Citra and Fort McCoy properties needs larger-helix anchors to hold their rated load.

**Access under the home.** If your skirting has to come off and go back on, that adds time. Same story if the crawl space is tight, wet, or full of old debris. A clean, dry, accessible underside is the cheapest underside.

**Frame ties vs. over-the-top ties.** Most homes built after mid-1976 use frame ties that connect the steel I-beams to ground anchors. Some older homes used over-the-top straps that run across the roof. Retrofitting an older setup costs more, and if you own one, our post on [frame ties vs. over-the-top ties](/blog/frame-ties-vs-over-the-top-ties/) explains what inspectors want to see.

**Condition of existing hardware.** Sound anchors with rusted straps are a cheap fix. Rusted anchor shafts are not, because the anchor has to be replaced entirely. Our guide to [rusted mobile home anchors](/blog/rusted-mobile-home-anchors/) shows the difference between surface rust you can monitor and section loss that fails inspection.

**Leveling first.** Tie-downs get tensioned against a level home. If your home has settled, tensioning straps on an out-of-level frame locks the problem in place. Plenty of our tie-down jobs start with [releveling the home](/mobile-home-leveling-ocala/) so the straps do their job correctly.

## Florida Wind Zones and Why Your Anchor Count Isn't Optional

Florida divides the state into wind zones for manufactured homes, and Marion, Sumter, and Citrus counties sit in Wind Zone 2. That zone sets the minimum number and spacing of anchors your home must have. Coastal counties in Wind Zone 3 require even more.

This matters for cost because the anchor count isn't a matter of opinion. An installer quoting you 8 anchors for a double wide in Ocala is either planning a partial job or doesn't know the standard. FEMA's building science research on manufactured housing, available through the [FEMA Building Science branch](https://www.fema.gov/emergency-managers/risk-management/building-science), consistently shows that homes anchored to current standards perform dramatically better in high wind than homes with missing or degraded anchors.

The practical takeaway: don't shop for the quote with the fewest anchors. Shop for the quote that meets the spacing standard at a fair per-anchor price. If you want the deeper background on zones and spacing, we broke it down in [Florida wind zones for mobile homes](/blog/florida-wind-zones-mobile-homes/).

One more wrinkle. Homes built before June 1976 predate the HUD code entirely, and insurers treat them differently. Tie-downs still help, but expect the inspection and paperwork side to be more involved.

## Can You Cut the Cost With DIY Tie-Down Installation?

You can buy auger anchors at farm supply stores for $15 to $30 each, and strap kits aren't expensive. So why does professional installation cost $50 to $85 per anchor?

Because the hardware is the cheap part. The value is in the parts you can't see on a shelf:

- **Anchor selection for your soil.** An anchor rated for 4,725 pounds only hits that rating in soil that matches its classification. The wrong helix size in sandy soil can pull out at a fraction of its rating.
- **Correct angle and stabilizer plates.** Anchors installed vertically without stabilizer plates can rotate through the soil under load. This is one of the most common DIY mistakes we find.
- **Proper tension.** Straps need to be tight enough to work and even enough that they don't pull the frame out of level. Over-tensioning one side is a quiet way to create the door and floor problems we cover in [signs your mobile home needs leveling](/blog/signs-mobile-home-needs-leveling/).
- **Paperwork that counts.** In Florida, mobile home installation work is regulated, and an insurer or lender wants documentation from a licensed installer or engineer. A weekend of DIY anchor work that no inspector will sign off on saves you nothing.

Where DIY makes sense: keeping your skirting vents clear, keeping the crawl space dry, and doing a flashlight walk-around twice a year to spot loose straps or surface rust early. Catching a $35 strap problem before it becomes a $700 anchor problem is the best cost control there is.

Where it doesn't: installing, replacing, or tensioning anchors and straps. If a strap has snapped or an anchor is visibly leaning, stop there and call a pro. That hardware is under load, and it's not a safe place to learn.

## How to Get a Quote You Can Actually Trust

A good tie-down quote is boring and specific. Here's what it should include:

1. **A count of existing anchors** and how many are reusable.
2. **The number of new anchors**, broken out by type (frame, longitudinal, centerline).
3. **Per-anchor pricing** so you can compare across bidders.
4. **Whether skirting removal and reinstallation is included.**
5. **What documentation you get**, and whether it satisfies your insurer or lender.

Red flags: a price given over the phone without anyone going under the home, pressure to sign same-day, or a quote that's vague about anchor count. Marion County has seen its share of storm-chaser outfits, and vague quotes are their favorite tool.

Timing matters too. Tie-down work books up fast between June and November when insurance renewals and hurricane headlines collide. Getting the work done in winter or spring usually means faster scheduling and sometimes better pricing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to add tie-downs to an older mobile home?

For pre-1990 homes that need a full retrofit, budget $1,500 to $2,500 for a single wide and $2,500 to $3,800 for a double wide in the Ocala area. Older homes often need longitudinal anchors the original install never included, and rusted hardware usually can't be reused. A written estimate after an under-home inspection will pin the number down.

### Will insurance pay for tie-down installation or replacement?

Usually no. Insurers treat tie-downs as maintenance, so the cost falls on the homeowner. The exception is documented storm damage to an otherwise sound system, which may be covered under your policy. What insurance definitely does is require proof of adequate tie-downs, so the work pays for itself in insurability.

### How many tie-downs does a double wide need in Florida?

In Wind Zone 2, which includes Marion, Sumter, and Citrus counties, most double wides need 18 to 30 frame anchors depending on length, plus centerline anchoring per the installation standard. Exact counts come from anchor spacing rules in the Florida Administrative Code and the home's dimensions. An installer should count and show you, not estimate from the driveway.

### How long do mobile home tie-downs last?

Galvanized anchors and straps typically last 15 to 25 years in Central Florida, less in consistently damp soil or where the vapor barrier has failed. Straps usually rust out before anchors do. A quick visual check each spring and fall, plus a professional look during any 4-point inspection, will catch wear long before it fails.
