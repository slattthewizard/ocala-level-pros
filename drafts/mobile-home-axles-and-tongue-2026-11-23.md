# Mobile Home Axles, Wheels and Tongue: Remove Them or Leave Them?

Some mobile homes keep their axles, wheels and tongue after setup, and some have them taken off. Either way, the axles are not what holds the home up. The piers carry the weight on the steel frame. Whether the running gear stays or goes is mostly a question for your lender, your park, and your local rules, not a structural one.

This post covers what the axles, wheels and tongue are, what HUD's installation standards say and do not say about them, and what to check before you pay anyone to cut something off. We level, anchor and skirt homes. We do not move them, so we will point you to the right people for that part.

## What the Axles, Wheels and Tongue Are

A mobile home is built on a steel chassis so it can be towed to the site once. HUD's construction standards define the chassis as the entire transportation system, made up of the drawbar and coupling mechanism, the frame, the running gear assembly, and lights ([24 CFR 3280.902](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3280.902)). The same section defines two of the pieces you can see from the yard:

- **Drawbar and coupling mechanism.** The rigid assembly with a coupling on it that connects the home's frame to the towing vehicle. People call this the tongue or the hitch.
- **Running gear assembly.** The suspension springs, axles, bearings, wheels, hubs, tires, and brakes, with their related hardware.

The frame is the long steel beam structure under each section. The standards describe it as the rigid substructure that supports the home both during transport and on site, and that also provides the platform for the running gear and the drawbar.

## The Axles Are Not the Support

This is the part that matters most. On site, the home is supported by piers set on footings, under the main frame beams. HUD's installation standards describe piers as the part of the support system between the footing and the home, and they spell out spacing, height, caps and shims for them. The running gear is not part of that support system.

Axles and tires were sized for the trip. Under the transport rules, the design load for running gear is based on the home's gross weight minus the weight on the tongue, and it has to handle highway shock and vibration. That is a towing design, not a foundation. Tires sit in sun and ground moisture for years, rubber ages, and axles rust. If a home is resting on its axles and not on its piers, the load is on parts nobody designed to sit that way.

So when you look under your home, you should see piers under the beam. If the wheels are still on, they should hang clear and not bear weight. If they bear weight, or the home is leaning on the tires, the piers have settled or were never set right. Our post on [mobile home pier types](/blog/mobile-home-pier-types/) shows what proper support looks like, and the [permanent foundation vs piers](/blog/permanent-foundation-vs-piers/) post compares the options.

## What HUD's Rules Say About Removing Them

We read through HUD's installation standards (24 CFR Part 3285) for the words drawbar, hitch, tongue, wheel, axle, running gear, and towing. The standards do not tell the installer to remove the axles, wheels or tongue. They are silent on it. What they do say:

- The main frame must be supported on piers on footings, with a minimum clearance of 12 inches between the lowest member of the main frame (I-beam or channel beam) and the grade under all areas of the home ([24 CFR 3285.305](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.305)).
- Moving a home to the site is addressed as a recommendation, with the local jurisdiction informed first and any special transportation permits obtained ([24 CFR 3285.902](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.902)).

That is it for the running gear. Note the clearance rule. If an axle or tongue hangs lower than the I-beam, it could cut into the space under the home, which is one practical reason some owners ask about removal.

Removal, when it happens, usually comes from somewhere else: a lender, a loan program, an insurer, a park, or a local permit. Those can be stricter than HUD's installation standards.

## Why Some Owners Take Them Off

Common reasons:

- **Lender or loan program rules.** Some loans on a home that is treated as real property want a permanent foundation. Rules differ by lender and program, so ask yours what they want in writing. Our post on [FHA foundation certification for mobile homes](/blog/fha-foundation-certification-mobile-home/) explains how that process works in general.
- **Looks.** A tongue sticking out the front or a pair of tires under the skirt bothers some people.
- **Space and access.** Axles and wheels take room under the home, and they make crawling and inspections harder.
- **Skirting fit.** Wheels can get in the way of a clean skirting line, especially at the ends.
- **Park rules.** Some parks require the tongue and hitch to be removed or hidden.

None of those is a code requirement in the standards we reviewed. They are choices or requirements from someone else, so find out which you have before you spend money.

## Reasons to Leave Them Alone

There are good reasons to leave things as they are.

- **You might move the home.** If there is any chance the home gets towed again, the running gear and hitch are what make it movable. Putting them back is expensive, and the mover may not accept a home without them.
- **Cutting a steel frame is not casual work.** The tongue is attached to the frame that holds the home up. A bad cut, a weld that goes wrong, or damage to wiring and plumbing near the front is not a small repair.
- **Nobody required it.** If your lender, park, and county do not ask, there may be no reason to do it.
- **Resale.** Some buyers and lenders look for it, some do not. Ask before you assume.

## Who Does the Removal

We do not remove axles, wheels, or tongues, and we do not move homes. Here is who does:

- **Mobile home movers and transporters.** They handle the running gear and hitch, and they know the transport side. Many do removal as a service.
- **Licensed installers and setup contractors.** Whoever sets or resets the home in your area usually handles the axles and tongue as part of a setup.
- **A welder, for a cut-off job.** Cutting steel off a frame should be done by someone experienced with these chassis.

Ask them to confirm that the removal does not weaken the frame or interfere with the piers, tie-downs, wiring, or plumbing. If the lender needs paperwork afterward, ask the person doing the work to put it in writing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do mobile homes keep their axles?

Many do, and many have them removed. HUD's installation standards do not tell the installer to remove axles, wheels or the tongue. The piers hold the home up either way. If removal is required, it usually comes from a lender, a park, or a local rule.

### Should I remove the tongue from my mobile home?

Only if someone requires it or you want it gone. Check with your lender, your park, and your county first. If you may ever move the home again, keep it. Have a mover or an experienced welder do the cutting, because the tongue is part of the steel frame.

### Do the axles hold up a mobile home?

No. The home is supported by piers on footings under the main frame beams. Axles and wheels were built to tow the home once, and if the home is resting on them, the piers need attention.

### Can you remove my axles and wheels?

No. We level, anchor and skirt homes, and we do not move homes or remove running gear. A mobile home mover or the setup contractor in your area can do it. We can check the piers, anchors, and clearance before or after.

### Do I need the wheels removed for an FHA loan?

Loan rules differ by lender and program, and we cannot speak for yours. Ask the lender what they require in writing. Some lenders ask for a permanent foundation certification, and our FHA foundation certification post explains the general process.
