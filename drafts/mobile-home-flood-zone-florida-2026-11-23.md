# Mobile Home in a Flood Zone in Florida: Anchoring, Elevation and Piers

If your mobile home sits in a mapped flood hazard area, the rules for how it is anchored and supported are stricter than for a home on high ground. HUD's installation standards say that in flood hazard areas, homes must be installed on foundation supports designed and anchored to prevent floatation, collapse or lateral movement. Standard piers and straps may not be enough.

We anchor, tie down and relevel homes in Marion, Sumter and Citrus counties. We don't design elevation projects, build flood foundations or sell flood insurance. This post explains what the federal text says, how to find out whether your lot is in a flood zone, and where a leveling and tie-down crew fits in.

## First Question: Is Your Lot in a Flood Zone?

Don't guess from how the yard looks. Flood zones come from maps, and they don't always match what you see. A lot can be in a mapped zone and stay dry for years, and a lot outside one can still take on water in a bad storm.

To check, use FEMA's flood map service center and search your address. You can also ask the county's building or floodplain office, or your insurance agent. Marion County has rivers, lakes and low areas, so some lots here are mapped as flood hazard areas and many are not. We won't guess about your street. Look it up.

If the map puts your home in a special flood hazard area, ask the local office what that means for repairs and changes. It can affect permits, what you may do to the foundation, and what insurance costs.

## What HUD's Installation Standards Say

Three sections of the installation standard deal with flood hazard areas. Here is what each one says.

[24 CFR 3285.302](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.302) says that in flood hazard areas, foundations, anchorings and support systems must be capable of resisting loads associated with design flood and wind events or combined wind and flood events. It also says homes must be installed on foundation supports that are designed and anchored to prevent floatation, collapse or lateral movement of the structure. And it says the manufacturer's installation instructions must indicate whether the foundation specifications have been designed for flood-resistant considerations, or are not designed to address flood loads.

[24 CFR 3285.406](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.406) is short. It says to refer to 3285.302 for anchoring requirements in flood hazard areas. So the anchoring rules for floods are the ones in 3285.302.

[24 CFR 3285.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.102) covers the initial installation of new manufactured homes in flood hazard areas. It says the installer must determine whether the site lies wholly or partly within a special flood hazard area, using the map the local authority has adopted. It also says homes in those areas must be installed on foundations engineered to incorporate methods and practices that minimize flood damage during the base flood, in line with local requirements and the referenced FEMA rules.

One limit to keep in mind: 3285.102 is written for new installations. If you own an older home that was set years ago, it does not tell you how to retrofit. Your local office and a licensed engineer do that.

## What This Means for Piers and Anchors

A standard pier and strap system holds a home against wind and keeps it up against gravity. Floodwater pushes in other ways. Moving water pushes sideways, standing water can lift, and fast water can scour soil out from under a footing.

Here is how that plays out under a home:

- **Piers.** Stacked block piers on pads can be pushed or undermined. Flood foundations are designed for those loads, and the manufacturer says whether the foundation is designed for flood loads.
- **Anchors and straps.** In a flood area the anchoring has to resist floatation and lateral movement as well as wind. Our post on [Florida mobile home tie-down requirements](/blog/florida-mobile-home-tie-down-requirements/) covers the wind side.
- **Soil.** Wet sand loses strength, and scour around a pier or anchor can remove the soil that holds it. Anchors that were fine when set can end up loose after a wet season.
- **Appliances.** Section 3285.102 also says appliances installed on the site in flood hazard areas must be anchored and elevated to or above the lowest floor of the home.

If your home was set before it was clear which rules applied, or you are not sure the foundation was designed for flood loads, the manufacturer's installation instructions are the first place to look, and the data plate tells you who built the home so you can ask for them. Our post on the [HUD data plate](/blog/mobile-home-hud-data-plate/) explains where to find it.

## Elevation and Flood Vents

Elevating a home means raising it so that the lowest floor sits above the flood level. That is an engineering and permitting job, and it usually means a new foundation. We don't do it. Look for a licensed engineer and a contractor who has done elevation work on manufactured homes, and talk to your local office first.

Flood vents are openings in an enclosure that let water flow through instead of building up pressure on a wall. Whether your area requires them is a local rule. We won't tell you whether your skirting needs them, because that depends on your zone and local rules. Ask the local office. Our post on [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/) covers the ordinary ventilation openings, and the two are not the same thing.

## After Heavy Rain or a Flood

Rain on its own is not a flood, but it can still move a home. After a wet stretch or high water, check for these signs:

- Doors and windows that stick or won't latch
- New gaps at trim, or cracks in walls and ceilings
- Piers that lean, or pads that have shifted or washed out
- Straps that hang slack
- Skirting that has bowed, pushed out or fallen
- A damp belly or standing water under the home

Our guide to [mobile home settling after rain](/blog/mobile-home-settling-after-rain/) goes into why this happens in sandy soil. If the home took on floodwater inside, the problem is bigger than level. Call your insurer and a restoration contractor first. Once the water is out and the structure is safe, we can check level and anchoring.

If you have to go under the home, don't do it while the ground is soaked or the home is held up by anything temporary. Never get under a home supported only by a jack.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my mobile home is in a flood zone?

Search your address on FEMA's flood map service center, or ask the Marion County building or floodplain office or your insurance agent. Don't judge by how the yard looks. A mapped flood hazard area and a yard that floods are not always the same thing.

### What does HUD require for a mobile home in a flood zone?

HUD's installation standards say that in flood hazard areas, foundations, anchorings and support systems must resist design flood and wind loads, and homes must be on supports designed and anchored to prevent floatation, collapse or lateral movement. The manufacturer's instructions must say whether the foundation is designed for flood loads.

### Can you elevate a mobile home in a flood zone?

Not us. Elevating a home is an engineering and permitting job that usually needs a new foundation and a contractor with that experience. We level homes and set tie-downs and anchors. We can check the current piers and anchors and write up what we find.

### Do standard tie-downs work in a flood zone?

Not always. Standard straps and anchors are built mainly for wind. Flood areas have to resist floatation and sideways movement too, and the home's installation instructions say whether the foundation was designed for flood loads. Check the data plate and the manufacturer's instructions, and ask the local office what applies.
