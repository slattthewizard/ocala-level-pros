# Soil Bearing Capacity Under a Mobile Home: Why Sand Matters

Soil bearing capacity is how much weight a patch of ground can carry before it squeezes, shifts, or sinks. Under a mobile home, every pier sits on a footing, and every footing sits on soil. If the soil is too weak for the load, the pier settles and the home goes out of level. HUD's installation standards list a bearing value for each soil type, and they range from 1,000 psf for loose sand to 4,000 psf or more for rock.

Marion County sits on a lot of sand, and sand behaves very differently dry and wet. This post explains what the rules say about soil, how soil is classified, what a pocket penetrometer does, and why a pier pad that looked fine at setup can drop a year or two later.

## What Soil Bearing Capacity Means

Psf is pounds per square foot. If the soil under a footing is rated at 1,500 psf, each square foot of footing can safely put 1,500 pounds on the ground. A bigger footing spreads the same load over more soil, which is why pad size and soil go together. HUD's [section 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312) says the sizing and layout of footings depends on the load-bearing capacity of the soil, the footings, and the piers.

So soil is half the equation. A pad that works fine on firm, compacted ground may be too small for the same pier on loose sand. Our post on [mobile home pier pads](/blog/mobile-home-pier-pads/) covers the pad side of it.

## What the Rules Say About the Ground

Two sections set the baseline.

**Firm ground.** [Section 3285.201](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.201) says that to help prevent settling or sagging, the foundation must be built on firm, undisturbed soil, or on fill compacted to at least 90 percent of its maximum relative density. Grass, roots, twigs, and wood scraps have to come out of the footing areas. Rotting roots and buried scrap leave voids as they decay, and the pad above sinks into them.

**Know the soil first.** [Section 3285.202](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.202) says the soil classification and bearing capacity must be determined before the foundation is constructed and anchored. The text gives several ways to do it:

- Soil tests done to generally accepted engineering practice.
- Soil records from the local authority having jurisdiction.
- The table of soil classifications and bearing capacities, if the soil type can be identified but the class or capacity cannot be determined by test or records.
- A pocket penetrometer.
- Or, in place of the table methods, an allowable pressure of 1,500 psf, unless site-specific information calls for lower values based on soil classification and type.

If the soil looks like peat, organic clay, or uncompacted fill, or has unusual conditions, the standards say a registered professional geologist, engineer, or architect must determine the classification and the maximum allowable bearing capacity.

## The Soil Classes and What Each Can Carry

The table in 3285.202 sorts soil into classes. The allowable bearing pressure for each:

| Class | Soil type, as the table describes it | Allowable pressure |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rock or hard pan | 4,000 psf or more |
| 2 | Sandy gravel and gravel, very dense or cemented sands, and similar | 2,000 psf |
| 3 | Sand, silty sand, clayey sand, medium dense coarse sands, very stiff silt and clays | 1,500 psf |
| 4A | Loose to medium dense sands, firm to stiff clays and silts, alluvial fills | 1,000 psf |
| 4B | Loose sands, firm clays, alluvial fills | 1,000 psf |
| 5 | Uncompacted fill, peat, organic clays | Refer to the 1,500 psf paragraph and the engineer requirement above |

The table's own footnote adds a catch: the values have not been adjusted for overburden pressure, embedment depth, water table height, or settlement problems. In other words, the number is a starting point, and a wet site can do worse than the number says.

Look at where sand falls. Dense, packed sand lands in the middle of the chart. Loose sand lands at 1,000 psf, the bottom of the usable range. Which one you have under your home takes a test or an inspection, because loose and dense sand look the same from above.

## How Soil Gets Tested

Sections 3285.202 and the ground anchor rules mention a few tools:

- **Soil test or boring.** A lab or engineer classifies the soil and reads blow counts, using standard test methods. This is the most accurate and the least common on a repair job.
- **Soil records.** The county may already have soil data for your area. [Section 3285.202](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.202) allows the local jurisdiction's records.
- **Torque probe.** A long steel rod with a helix on the end is turned with a torque wrench. The table lists torque readings for each class, such as more than 550 inch-pounds for Class 2 and 175 to 275 for Class 4B. It is mostly used for ground anchors.
- **Pocket penetrometer.** This is a small handheld gauge that you press into the soil to get a rough strength reading. It is quick and cheap. In general it is better suited to clay than to loose sand, which is one reason a crew checks the ground by more than one method.

For a typical relevel, nobody pulls a lab sample. A crew probes around the pads, looks at how deep the pad has sunk, and judges the soil from what it sees. If the ground looks unusual or soft, or the pads keep dropping, that is the time to bring in an engineer.

## Why Marion County Sand Causes Settling

Our local soil is mostly sand, and that has some specific habits.

**It washes.** Florida summer storms drop a lot of rain fast. Water moving under and around a pad carries sand with it, and the pad loses its bed on one side. The pier tips and the home sags there.

**It loosens when it dries and rewets.** Sand does not hold together on its own. Cycles of soaking and drying shake the grains down and leave voids, so pads settle over time instead of all at once.

**It is poor at holding water in place.** After a heavy rain, standing water under a home soaks the footing area for days. Water table height is one of the things the HUD table says its values do not account for.

**It hides what is underneath.** A pad can look level on top while the sand below has dropped away. That is why we check by probing and by looking at the level of the home, not only at the pad.

## Drainage and Moisture: Keeping Water Off the Soil

Weak soil gets weaker when it stays wet, so the standards deal with water in two places.

[Section 3285.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.203) requires drainage that directs surface water away from the home to protect against erosion of foundation supports and to prevent water build-up under the home. Drainage must slope a minimum of one-half inch per foot away from the foundation for the first ten feet. Where something prevents that slope, the site needs drains, swales, or other grading to carry water away. If the home has gutters and downspouts, the runoff has to be directed away from the home.

[Section 3285.204](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.204) covers ground moisture. If the space under the home is enclosed with skirting or other materials, a vapor retarder must cover the ground under the home, unless the home is in an arid region with dry soil. The material is at least 6-mil polyethylene or equal, joints overlap at least 12 inches, and voids or tears must be repaired. That sheet does not make the soil stronger, but it helps keep the ground from staying saturated. Our [vapor barrier service](/vapor-barrier-ocala/) is what we do for that.

Roof runoff dumping next to a pier is a common reason a single pad sinks. A downspout that ends beside the skirting can wash out one spot over a few storms.

## Soil Bearing Versus Sinkholes

Bearing capacity is about the top few feet of ground under the pads. A sinkhole is a different problem, where the ground drops from far below. Both can drop a home, but the signs differ. ## What To Do If Your Pads Keep Settling

1. Check drainage first. Look for water pooling near the home and fix the grade or gutter outlets.
2. Look at the pads. Sunk, tilted, or cracked pads point to soil that cannot carry the load at that pad size.
3. Get the level checked. A written leveling inspection shows which piers dropped and by how much.
4. Ask whether a bigger pad or a different footing makes sense at the spots that keep dropping, and whether the soil needs an engineer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the soil bearing capacity for a mobile home?

HUD's installation standards list allowable bearing pressure by soil class: 4,000 psf or more for rock, 2,000 psf for Class 2, 1,500 psf for Class 3, and 1,000 psf for Classes 4A and 4B, which include loose sands. In place of testing or the table, the standards allow 1,500 psf to be used unless site information calls for lower values.

### How do I test the soil under my mobile home?

The standards allow soil tests to accepted engineering practice, local soil records, the classification table, or a pocket penetrometer. If the soil looks like peat, organic clay, or uncompacted fill, a registered geologist, engineer, or architect has to determine it.

### Why do pier pads sink in sandy soil?

Loose sand washes out under pads during heavy rain and shakes down in repeated wet and dry cycles. A pad that is too small for the soil, or sits where roof water drains, settles faster. The pier drops with it and the home goes out of level.

### Does a mobile home need a soil test before setup?

The standards say the soil classification and bearing capacity must be determined before the foundation is built and anchored. That can be done by test, by records, by the table, by a pocket penetrometer, or by using 1,500 psf where nothing lower is called for. Your local jurisdiction may have its own requirements.

### Can a bigger pier pad fix settling?

Sometimes. A larger footing spreads the load over more soil, which helps where the ground is weak but sound. It does not fix soil that keeps washing out or sits under pooled water, so drainage has to be dealt with too.
