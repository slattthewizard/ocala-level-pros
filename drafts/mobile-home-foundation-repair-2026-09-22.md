# Mobile Home Foundation Repair: What Actually Gets Fixed Under a Pier-Set Home

Plenty of Marion County owners search for mobile home foundation repair right after a door stops latching or a corner starts feeling low. The phrase makes most people picture a house: poured concrete, slab cracks, steel piers driven deep into the ground. Almost none of that applies here, because most mobile and manufactured homes around Ocala don't sit on that kind of foundation at all. They sit on piers.

## What "Foundation" Means on a Pier-Set Mobile Home

A pier-set mobile home's foundation is a system, not a slab. Working from the ground up:

- **Footings (pads).** Concrete pads spread each pier's load into the soil so it doesn't punch straight down into loose Marion County sand.
- **Piers.** Stacks that carry the home's weight between the footing and the steel frame. Around here that's usually concrete block, though steel and ABS plastic piers show up too.
- **Caps and shims.** A cap sits on top of the pier, and wood or plastic shims take up the last fraction of an inch to bring the frame level.
- **The steel frame.** The I-beam chassis the whole home rides on. In a standard set-up it rests on the piers rather than being bolted to them.
- **Anchors and straps.** Ground anchors tied to the frame with galvanized steel strapping keep the home from shifting or lifting in wind.
- **The ground and drainage around it.** Grading, gutters, and how water moves through the crawl space affect every part of the system above.

Skirting deserves a separate mention: it isn't structural. Vinyl, metal, or rigid-foam skirting closes in the crawl space and finishes the look, but it carries none of the home's weight and fixes nothing about a low corner or a leaning pier.

Compare that to a permanent foundation: a poured perimeter stem wall or a slab the home sits on, which is a different construction method entirely, usually built when a home is first set or retrofitted for FHA financing. Everything below is about the pier system almost every Marion County mobile home actually has.

## Symptom to Cause: What Your Home Is Telling You

Most owners notice a symptom inside the home long before they know what's happening underneath. This table maps what you're seeing to what's usually behind it.

| What you notice | What it usually points to |
|---|---|
| Floors that slope toward one end or corner | Settled piers, or a footing that's sunk into soft soil |
| Doors sticking or not latching | The frame has racked out of level, often from one or two low piers |
| Cracks at door and window corners | Frame movement stressing the drywall at its weakest points |
| A gap or step at the double-wide marriage line | One half of the home has settled more than the other |
| A corner that feels lower than the rest | A localized pier or footing failure under that corner |
| A leaning or visibly cracked pier | The pier itself has failed and can't be shimmed back to level |
| Loose, rusted, or missing straps | The anchor system has degraded and needs its own inspection |
| Water pooling or damp soil under the home | A drainage problem likely softening footings and rotting shims |

None of these symptoms tell you the fix on their own. A sloped floor from an ordinary settled pier and a sloped floor from a genuinely failed one feel identical standing in the hallway. That's why an inspection looks under the home instead of guessing from what you can feel through your feet. ## The Repairs, Explained

"Foundation repair" on a pier-set home isn't one job. It's whichever of these actually matches what failed.

### Reshim and Relevel

The simplest fix. A crew jacks the frame just enough to take the load off, adjusts or replaces the shims and caps, and brings the home back to level. This handles ordinary settling where the piers themselves are still sound, just sitting a little low or slightly out of adjustment.

### Re-Stack or Replace a Failed Pier

When a pier is cracked, leaning, or has separated from the frame, shims can't fix it. The pier gets re-stacked square if the blocks are still sound, or replaced outright if they aren't. We cover how to tell the difference in [when to replace mobile home piers](/blog/when-to-replace-mobile-home-piers/), and the block, steel, and ABS options in [mobile home pier types](/blog/mobile-home-pier-types/).

### Replace a Cracked or Sunken Footing

If the pad under a pier has cracked or punched down into the soil, the pier above it will keep settling no matter how many times it gets releveled. HUD's installation standard for these footings, 24 CFR § 3285.312, requires a footing on undisturbed soil or fill compacted to 90 percent of maximum relative density, a footing under every pier, and either a 4-inch precast concrete pad or at least a 6-inch poured pad, slab, or ribbon. Replacing a failed footing means digging it out, correcting the soil underneath, and setting a new pad to that spec before the pier goes back on. Our guide to [mobile home pier pads and footings](/blog/mobile-home-pier-pads/) has more detail, and you can read [the full text of § 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312) yourself.

### Anchors and Straps

Piers hold the home up. Anchors and straps hold it down and in place against wind. That's a separate system that can need attention even when the piers are fine, especially on older homes where straps have rusted through or anchors were never installed to current spec. See [mobile home tie-downs in Ocala](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/) for what that work involves.

### Fix the Water First

None of the above holds up if water keeps pooling under the home. Standing water softens the soil under footings, rots wood shims, and rusts steel hardware faster than anything else down there. If that's part of your picture, read [standing water under a mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/) before you pay for pier or footing work, because fixing the drainage protects whatever else gets repaired.

## Why a House Foundation-Repair Company Might Not Be the Right Call

A lot of foundation-repair companies advertising in Central Florida are set up for site-built houses: poured slabs, block stem walls, and problems solved with push piers, helical piles, or slab jacking. That's a different structural system solving a different problem. A pier-set mobile home doesn't have a slab to lift or a stem wall to stabilize. It has a grid of individual piers, each one an independent load path back to its own footing.

That mismatch shows up in the estimate. A house-foundation crew may not carry shims sized for a mobile home frame, may not work with HUD's pier and footing standards day to day, and may price the job around methods built for a house problem, not a mobile home one. That's not a knock on those companies; their tools and training are built for a different structure entirely. It just means the fit matters more than the size of the truck or the length of the review list. If you're collecting quotes, ask directly what's under your home right now (piers, not a slab) and whether the estimate actually reflects that.

## When It's Not a Routine Repair: Sinkhole Signs

Most settling in Marion County is ordinary: sandy soil compacting under load, made worse by a wet summer. But this area also sits in Florida's sinkhole belt, and every so often what looks like routine settling is something else.

Treat it as a signal to get a proper look before anyone relevels anything if you notice:

- Sudden sinking, rather than the slow settling that develops over months or years
- A depression forming in the yard, especially near the home
- Cracks, inside or outside, that keep growing rather than staying the same size
- Doors and windows going out of square quickly, over days instead of gradually

Releveling a home sitting over active ground movement doesn't fix the underlying problem, and it can mask what's happening long enough for it to get worse. If you notice any of the signs above, our post on [sinkhole signs Marion County mobile home owners should watch for](/blog/sinkhole-signs-marion-county/) goes into more detail, and the [Florida Geological Survey's sinkhole page](https://floridadep.gov/fgs/sinkholes) is the primary source on how sinkholes form and what the state recommends.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a mobile home foundation be repaired, or does it need replacing?

Almost everything under a pier-set home can be repaired in place: reshimming, releveling, re-stacking or swapping individual piers, replacing a cracked footing, or renewing anchors and straps. Replacing the whole pier system at once is the exception, usually saved for old setups where block failure is widespread. Most jobs fix specific piers or footings, not the entire system at once.

### How much does mobile home foundation repair cost?

It depends entirely on which part failed. A single-corner relevel runs $300 to $600, a full double-wide relevel runs $700 to $1,500, and pier or footing replacement is priced per pier once someone has actually looked underneath. The estimate itself is free, and if you want a full written inspection report on its own, that runs $150 to $250.

### Does homeowners insurance cover mobile home foundation repair?

Usually not for ordinary settling, which most policies treat as maintenance rather than a covered loss. Coverage is more likely to apply to sudden damage tied to a specific event, like storm damage, than to the slow soil movement behind most Marion County settling. Check your specific policy or ask your carrier directly rather than assuming either way.

### How do I know if my mobile home foundation is failing?

Watch for floors that slope, doors that stick or won't latch, cracks forming at door and window corners, a gap opening at the double-wide marriage line, or a pier you can see leaning or cracked through skirting vents. Any of those is worth a proper inspection rather than a guess, since a settled pier and a failed one can feel identical from inside the house. Check for loose or rusted straps too, since the anchor system can fail independently of the piers.
