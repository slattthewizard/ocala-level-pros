## Who Sets Florida's Tie-Down Rules (and Why They Exist)

Two levels of government share this job.

The federal side is HUD. Every manufactured home built since June 1976 was built to the HUD Code, and homes built after July 1994 were built to updated wind standards that divide the country into wind zones. You can read the actual installation standards at the [U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/rmra/mhs/mhshome) if you enjoy that sort of thing.

The state side is the Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles, which regulates mobile home installation through Rule 15C-1 of the Florida Administrative Code. That rule covers anchors, straps, spacing, piers, and who's allowed to do the work. Florida requires installers to be licensed by the state, and you can verify a license through [FLHSMV](https://www.flhsmv.gov/motor-vehicles-tags-titles/mobile-homes/).

Here's the plain-English version of why the rules exist. A mobile home sitting on piers is heavy, but it's not attached to the earth. Wind doesn't just push on a home, it lifts it, the same way air lifts an airplane wing. Tie-downs are steel straps that run from the home's frame (and sometimes over the roof) to anchors screwed four feet or more into the ground. They turn "sitting on blocks" into "connected to the ground."

That connection is what the state inspects, what insurance companies ask about, and what we spend a lot of our week fixing.

## Florida Mobile Home Tie-Down Requirements by Wind Zone

Florida has no Wind Zone I. The entire state falls into Wind Zone II or Wind Zone III under the HUD map, which is stricter than most of the country.

Here's how it shakes out locally:

- **Wind Zone III** covers South Florida and a coastal strip along most of the state's shoreline.
- **Wind Zone II** covers inland Florida, including Ocala, Marion County, Sumter County, and inland Citrus County.

Your zone matters for two reasons. First, homes built after July 1994 have a data plate (usually inside a kitchen cabinet or near the electrical panel) that states which zone the home was built for. A Zone II home can't legally be installed in a Zone III area. Second, your zone drives how many anchors and straps the installation needs. Zone III installs need more of everything, spaced closer together.

If you want the full breakdown of how zones affect your home, we wrote a separate guide on [Florida wind zones for mobile homes](/blog/florida-wind-zones-mobile-homes/).

One more wrinkle: homes built before June 1976 predate the HUD Code entirely. They can still be anchored, and they should be, but they often need over-the-top straps because their frames weren't designed for frame-tie loads. If you own a pre-1976 home, get a pro's eyes on it before assuming any standard spacing chart applies.

### What the rules require in our area

For a typical Zone II home around Ocala, a compliant setup generally includes:

- Ground anchors driven to full depth, with stabilizer plates in most soil conditions
- Galvanized steel straps rated for the load, connected to the home's steel frame
- Diagonal frame ties along both long sides of the home
- Longitudinal ties (running the home's length) to resist front-to-back movement, required on homes installed under the modern rules
- Vertical ties or over-the-top straps where the home's design calls for them

Exact counts come from the home's installation manual or the state's tables. A single wide in Zone II commonly needs somewhere in the range of 10 to 14 frame ties per side, but that's a ballpark, not a substitute for the spec.

## Anchors, Straps, and Spacing: What the Code Actually Asks For

Let's get concrete about the hardware, because this is where inspections pass or fail.

**Anchors.** The standard ground anchor is a steel shaft, usually four feet long, with helix blades that screw into the soil. In Central Florida's sandy soil, stabilizer plates matter a lot. A stabilizer plate is a flat steel plate driven in beside the anchor head that keeps the anchor from slicing sideways through sand under load. Around here, an anchor without one is often an anchor that moves.

**Straps.** Code calls for galvanized steel strapping, typically 1.25 inches wide, rated with the anchor as a system. Straps must be tight, not rusted through, and not spliced with baling wire or ratchet straps from the hardware store. Yes, we've seen both.

**Spacing.** Frame ties are spaced along the home's main steel beams, generally no more than about 5 feet 4 inches apart in Zone II installs, closer in Zone III. The ends of the home need ties near each corner. Longitudinal ties go at the ends of the home to stop it from sliding lengthwise.

**Connections.** Every strap connects to the anchor with a bolt-and-buckle tensioning head. The strap wraps the frame beam or attaches with an approved connector, never hooked over a bolt head or wrapped around plumbing.

## Simple Checks You Can Do This Weekend

You don't need to be a contractor to spot the obvious stuff. With a flashlight and ten minutes at the skirting access panel, look for:

1. **Rust at the soil line.** Anchors fail where steel meets dirt. Deep orange scale or flaking metal is a red flag.
2. **Slack straps.** A healthy strap is tight enough to hum when you tap it. If it wiggles or droops, it's not doing its job.
3. **Missing straps.** Straps should appear at regular intervals down both long sides. Big gaps mean something's missing.
4. **Tilted anchor heads.** An anchor leaning hard toward the home has probably pulled through the soil.
5. **Improvised hardware.** Ratchet straps, chains, rope, or rebar stakes are not tie-downs. If you see them, someone cut corners.

Now the important part: know when to stop. Tightening a strap changes the load on every strap near it, and a corroded anchor can snap under tension. Crawling under a home with failing supports is genuinely dangerous, and DIY anchor work can void the documentation your insurer needs anyway. Look, don't wrench. If anything on that list shows up, close the panel and call someone licensed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do all mobile homes in Florida need tie-downs?

Yes. Florida requires every mobile and manufactured home to be anchored, regardless of age. Homes installed since the modern rules took effect must meet the current anchor and strap standards, and older homes are expected to be anchored to the standards in place when they were installed. In practice, insurers and buyers usually want older homes brought up to something close to current spec anyway.

### How often should mobile home tie-downs be inspected?

Have a professional look at them every three to five years, and after any hurricane or tropical storm that passes through the area. Central Florida's sandy soil and summer moisture are hard on buried steel, so problems build quietly. A quick flashlight check of your own once a year between professional visits is a smart habit.

### Can I install or tighten tie-downs myself in Florida?

Florida requires mobile home installation work, including anchoring, to be done by a licensed installer, so a DIY system won't satisfy the state or your insurance company. You're welcome to inspect visually and take photos. Leave tensioning and anchor replacement to a licensed pro, both for safety and because you'll need the documentation later.

### What happens if my tie-downs fail a 4-point inspection?

Usually the insurer gives you a window to fix the problems and submit proof, often photos and a contractor's invoice or an engineer's letter. If you don't respond, the policy can be declined or non-renewed. Most tie-down corrections around Ocala cost between a few hundred dollars and about $3,000, and once documented, coverage typically moves forward.
