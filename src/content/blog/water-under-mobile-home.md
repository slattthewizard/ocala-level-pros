## Why Water Collects Under a Mobile Home in Florida

Central Florida gets roughly 50 inches of rain a year, and most of it falls between June and September. The [Florida Climate Center](https://climatecenter.fsu.edu/products-services/data/statewide-averages/precipitation) tracks it, and the numbers are consistent: our summers dump more water on your lot in three months than some states see all year.

That rain has to go somewhere. When it ends up under your home instead of away from it, one of these culprits is almost always to blame.

**The lot slopes toward the home.** This is the big one. Your home site should be graded so water runs away from the foundation on all sides. Over the years, soil settles, flower beds get built up, and neighbors change their own drainage. A lot that drained fine in 2005 can pool water in 2026.

**No gutters, or downspouts that dump at the skirting.** A single-wide roof sheds hundreds of gallons in a hard storm. Without gutters, all of it lands in a drip line right at the edge of the home and soaks straight under the skirting. With gutters but short downspouts, you've just concentrated the same water into four spots.

**Skirting that traps water instead of blocking it.** Solid skirting with no working vents keeps moisture in as effectively as it keeps rain out. Once water gets under the home, it can't evaporate, so the crawl space stays wet long after the yard dries.

**Plumbing and AC condensate leaks.** A dripping supply line, a leaking drain joint, or an AC condensate line that terminates under the home can create a permanent wet spot that has nothing to do with rain. If the puddle is there in dry weather, think plumbing first.

**A high water table.** Parts of Marion County, especially low-lying lots near wetlands and retention areas, sit on soil that saturates from below during the wet season. You can't regrade your way out of a high water table, but you can manage it with drains and a sealed vapor barrier.

## What Standing Water Under Your Home Damages First

Water doesn't attack everything at once. It follows a fairly predictable order, and knowing that order tells you how urgent your situation is.

**First, the soil.** Dry Florida sand holds a surprising amount of weight. Saturated sand doesn't. When the ground under a pier stays wet for weeks, the pier pad can press down into it, and the pier sinks. One sinking pier shifts load to its neighbors, and now you've got doors that stick, cracks over window corners, and a floor that slopes toward one end of the house. We covered the warning signs in our post on [mobile home settling after rain](/blog/mobile-home-settling-after-rain/).

**Second, the vapor barrier and belly board.** Standing water raises the humidity under the home to near 100 percent. That moisture condenses on the belly wrap, works into tears and sags, and soaks the insulation. A soaked belly can hold water against your subfloor for months.

**Third, the subfloor.** Most mobile home subfloors are particleboard or OSB, and both turn to mush with prolonged moisture exposure. If you're feeling [soft spots in your mobile home floor](/blog/soft-spots-mobile-home-floor/) near exterior walls or under the bathroom, moisture from below is the usual suspect.

**Fourth, the steel.** Anchors, straps, and the frame itself all rust faster in a humid crawl space. That matters for more than strength. Florida insurance carriers ask for tie-down documentation, and rusted straps are a common reason a four-point inspection comes back with problems.

**Fifth, your air.** The EPA estimates that a meaningful share of indoor air comes up from the crawl space. Mold growing under the home doesn't stay under the home. The EPA's [guide to mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) is blunt about it: control the moisture or you'll never control the mold. If you're seeing or smelling it already, read our post on [mold under a mobile home](/blog/mold-under-mobile-home/) before you seal anything up.

## Puddle or Problem? How to Tell the Difference

Not every wet spot is an emergency. Here's a quick way to sort a normal rainy-season puddle from a chronic problem that's costing you money.

A one-time puddle shows up after heavy rain, shrinks visibly within 48 to 72 hours, and leaves the soil damp but firm. The vapor barrier above it looks intact and the piers around it sit straight.

A chronic problem looks different. The water is still there four or five days after the last rain. The soil stays soupy, you see algae or a green tint on the ground, and there's a musty smell when you open the skirting access panel. Piers near the wet zone may already show a lean, or you'll see fresh shimming gaps where the frame has lifted off a sunken pier.

Two more red flags worth a flashlight check. Look for water lines or staining on the piers themselves, which tell you how deep the water has stood in the past. And look up at the belly board for sagging pouches, which mean water has already gotten inside the wrap.

## How to Get Rid of Water Under a Mobile Home

Fixing this is a two-part job: get the water that's there out, then stop new water from getting in. Skipping the second part means you'll be doing the first part again by September.

**Step 1: Find the source on a dry day.** Walk the perimeter after two or three rain-free days. If the ground under the home is still wet, suspect plumbing, AC condensate, or a high water table. If it's dry until the next storm, you're looking at grading, gutters, or skirting gaps.

**Step 2: Pump or drain the standing water.** For a few inches of ponded water, a cheap utility pump or even a wet/dry vac through an access panel works. For recurring water, a shallow trench to daylight on the low side of the lot beats pumping every time it rains.

**Step 3: Fix the grading and gutters.** Soil should slope away from the home at least six inches over the first ten feet. Downspouts should discharge four to six feet from the skirting, farther if the lot is flat. This is the highest-value dollar you'll spend on this whole problem.

**Step 4: Add drainage if grading isn't enough.** French drains along the uphill side, a gravel curtain drain, or a small dry well handle lots where you can't change the slope. On high-water-table lots, a sump basin under the home is sometimes the honest answer.

**Step 5: Dry the crawl space and repair the vapor barrier.** Once the water's gone, the space needs airflow to dry, which means working skirting vents. Then the [vapor barrier](/vapor-barrier-ocala/) needs to be inspected, patched, or replaced so ground moisture stops migrating up into the belly. A torn or missing barrier will keep your subfloor damp even on a dry lot.

**Step 6: Check the piers and level.** Any home that's had standing water under it for more than a season should be checked for level. Sunken piers don't fix themselves when the ground dries. Our [mobile home leveling service in Ocala](/mobile-home-leveling-ocala/) includes a full pier-by-pier inspection, so you'll know exactly which footings moved and which are fine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal to have some water under a mobile home after rain?

A damp crawl space for a day or two after a heavy Florida storm is common and usually harmless. Standing water that ponds and stays for more than three or four days is not normal, and it means water is arriving faster than the site can shed it. That's the point where soil starts softening under your piers and you should track down the source.

### Will standing water under my mobile home cause it to become unlevel?

Yes, and it's one of the most common causes of settling we see in Marion County. Saturated sand compresses under the weight of the pier pads, letting individual piers sink while others stay put. Once the water problem is fixed, the home usually needs releveling because the sunken piers won't rise back on their own.

### Can I just put a dehumidifier under my mobile home?

A dehumidifier treats the symptom, not the source, and most units can't keep up with an open crawl space that has active water intrusion. Fix the drainage and the vapor barrier first. After the space is dry and sealed, ventilation through working skirting vents usually handles ongoing humidity without any equipment.

### Does homeowners insurance cover water damage under a mobile home?

Usually not for gradual problems. Most Florida policies cover sudden events like a burst pipe but exclude damage from long-term seepage, poor drainage, and deferred maintenance. That's another reason to fix drainage early: by the time standing water rots a subfloor or sinks a pier, you're likely paying out of pocket for all of it.
