Mobile home skirting vents look like trim, but they're the only thing moving air through the crawl space under your home. In Marion County's humidity, that job never takes a day off. Skip enough of them, or block the ones you have, and moisture backs up under the floor no matter how new the home is.

The number isn't a guess. HUD's installation standards set an exact minimum for how much net vent area a skirted home needs, based on the floor area above it, and that minimum changes if a ground vapor barrier is installed. Most owners have never seen the math, so they have no idea whether their skirting has enough.

This guide covers what the vents do, the federal rule that sets the minimum, the real math for a single-wide and a double-wide, and where the openings need to sit.

## What Skirting Vents Actually Do Under a Florida Home

Central Florida crawl spaces stay humid nearly year round, and the wet season from June through September adds standing groundwater and near-constant evaporation on top of that. Warm, moist air rises off the sand, hits the cooler underside of your home, and condenses on the ducts, the belly board, and the subfloor.

Vents give that air somewhere to go. A skirted crawl space with openings on opposite sides sets up cross-ventilation: air moves in one side, picks up moisture, and moves out the other before it soaks into wood. Without that airflow, the space under the home behaves like a sealed box in a swamp. Standing water under the home is a separate drainage problem vents alone won't fix, covered in [standing water under your mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/).

You'll usually smell the problem before you see it. A musty odor inside the home, near closets on exterior walls or floor registers, often traces back to stagnant air under the floor. Left alone, ducts sweat, insulation sags with absorbed moisture, and the subfloor softens in spots you can feel through the carpet. Mold follows once the wood stays damp long enough, a slower, messier fix than adding a vent ever is; we cover that in [mold and moisture under a mobile home](/blog/mold-under-mobile-home/). ## The HUD Rule for Mobile Home Skirting Ventilation

The vent count isn't a rule of thumb from a supply house. It's in HUD's federal installation standards, 24 CFR § 3285.505, the section on crawlspace ventilation. In plain terms:

- Minimum net vent area is **1 square foot for every 150 square feet** of floor area, with no ground cover.
- That drops to **1 square foot for every 1,500 square feet** of floor area where a uniform 6-mil polyethylene sheet (or other acceptable vapor retarder) covers the ground under the entire floor area.
- Openings must sit **as high as practicable** above the ground, not down at grade.
- Openings must sit on **at least two opposite sides** so air crosses the crawl space instead of pooling on one side.
- Every opening needs a **perforated, corrosion- and weather-resistant cover** that keeps rodents out. Adjustable, closeable covers are only required in areas subject to freezing.
- At least one **access opening**, not less than 18 inches wide and 24 inches high and not less than 3 square feet, has to sit where utility connections under the home stay reachable.
- The **dryer vent** and any combustion air inlet must pass through the skirting to the outside, and drainage from the AC, furnace, or water heater must be directed away from under the home.

You can read the section straight from the source at [eCFR § 3285.505](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505). It's short, and reading it takes two minutes if you're comparing quotes or checking work already done.

## How Many Vents Your Home Needs: The Math

The HUD rule gives you a total net vent area for the whole home, not a vent count. You get the number of individual vents by dividing that total by the net free area of whichever vent you're using, which is smaller than its outside dimensions because of the louvers and screen.

For two common home sizes, the totals work out like this:

| Home | Floor Area | Net Vent Area (No Ground Cover) | Net Vent Area (With Full 6-Mil Ground Cover) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14x70 single-wide | 980 sq ft | about 6.5 sq ft (about 941 sq in) | about 0.65 sq ft (about 94 sq in) |
| 28x60 double-wide | 1,680 sq ft | about 11.2 sq ft (about 1,613 sq in) | about 1.12 sq ft (about 161 sq in) |

That "net vent area" figure is where most people go wrong. A vent that measures 16 by 8 inches on the outside doesn't give you 128 square inches of airflow: the frame, louvers, and screen block a chunk of it. Manufacturers print the actual net free area on the vent or its packaging, usually in square inches, and that's the number to divide into your target, not the outer dimensions.

Say your single-wide needs about 941 square inches without a ground cover, and the vents you're looking at are rated for roughly 50 square inches of net free area each. That's around 19 vents, spread around the perimeter and weighted toward two opposite sides. A different vent size changes the count, so there's no shortcut around checking the printed rating.

A full ground vapor barrier drops that legal minimum to a tenth of the figure, a real difference on a materials list. More on that trade-off below.

## Where Skirting Vents Belong (and Where They Don't)

Total square inches only matter if the vents are placed so air actually crosses the space. A pile of vents bunched on one side does almost nothing for the far corners.

Spread openings around the full perimeter, with vents on at least two opposite sides. Corners deserve attention too, since that's where airflow stalls first, especially under additions, bump-outs, or the marriage line on a double-wide. Keep vents as high on the skirting panel as the design allows. That's what the rule asks for, and it keeps them clear of splash-back, mulch and soil.

What quietly cancels out a correctly sized vent:

- **AC condenser units** parked directly in front of a vent block most of its airflow.
- **Shrubs and landscaping** planted against the skirting do the same thing over a season or two of growth.
- **Mulch piles or soil banked against the skirting**, common after landscaping work or storm cleanup, can bury a vent below grade entirely.
- **Stored items** leaned against skirting panels from inside a shed or under a porch.

None of this shows up in a vent count on paper. A home can have the right net free area on paper and still trap moisture because half the vents are sealed by whatever's sitting in front of them. Walking the perimeter once a year and clearing anything near each vent costs nothing.

## Types of Skirting Vents, in Plain Terms

You don't need a brand name to pick the right vent, just the three basic types installers use.

**Fixed louvered vents** are the standard choice. Slanted slats keep rain out while air passes through, and there's nothing to open or close. They're the usual pick in Central Florida, where the job is moving air most of the year.

**Screened vents** pair a louvered or open frame with a fine mesh rodent screen behind it, which HUD requires on every opening regardless of type. The screen is what stops mice, rats, and snakes from using the vent as a front door. Screens degrade from sun and weather, so a vent that looks fine from a few feet away can still have a torn screen behind it.

**Automatic temperature-operated vents** open and close on their own using a temperature-sensitive coil. They matter in climates where the crawl space needs to seal up against freezing pipes, which is why HUD requires an adjustable, closeable cover only in areas subject to freezing.

Marion County does get the odd freezing night, but cold snaps here are short. For most of the year the job is the opposite of a cold-climate home: keep vents open, unobstructed, and screened. ## Why a Ground Vapor Barrier Lowers the Vent Minimum (and Why That's Not a Shortcut)

Look at the math above again: a full 6-mil ground vapor barrier cuts the legal minimum vent area to a tenth of what an uncovered dirt crawl space needs. There's a reason for that. A big share of the moisture in a crawl space comes up out of the ground itself, so covering that source with plastic leaves less vapor for the vents to carry away.

It's a good trade. A proper ground vapor barrier cuts moisture at the source instead of just venting it after the fact, and it lowers what code requires in vent openings. See [the best vapor barrier for a mobile home](/blog/best-vapor-barrier-mobile-home/) for materials and pricing; our [vapor barrier and underbelly service](/vapor-barrier-ocala/) covers both spot repairs and full replacements.

One catch: the reduced number is a legal minimum, not a target. It tells you how few vents you're allowed to have, not how few you should have. Don't treat a vapor barrier as a reason to close up or remove vents already there. The barrier handles ground moisture, not humidity in the outside air that still needs to move through the crawl space, and it does nothing once it's torn, punctured, or installed with gaps around the piers.

## A 5-Minute Check You Can Do From Outside

You don't need to go under the home to get a read on your vents. Walk the perimeter with these five things in mind:

- **Count them.** Compare what you find to the math above for your home size, using the net free area printed on the vents if you can still read it.
- **Look for crushed or blocked vents.** Vinyl and lightweight metal vents dent from mowers, weed trimmers, and items stored against the skirting. A crushed vent has less open area than its rating assumes.
- **Check the screens.** Torn or rusted-through screening still lets air through but stops nothing else, which is an open invitation for animals looking for a dry place to den up. More on that in [keeping animals out from under a mobile home](/blog/animals-under-mobile-home/).
- **Follow the dryer vent.** It should run through the skirting to the outside, not dump lint and warm moist air into the crawl space.
- **Check the AC condensate line.** It should drain away from the skirting, not onto the ground under a vent or against the panel.

None of this requires tools or crawling under the home. If something looks off from the outside, that's the cue to get someone under there with a flashlight rather than guess.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many vents does a single-wide mobile home need?

It depends on the floor area and whether a ground vapor barrier is installed. A 14x70 single-wide, at 980 square feet, needs about 6.5 square feet of net vent area without a ground cover, or about 0.65 square feet with a full 6-mil cover. Divide that target by the net free area printed on your chosen vent.

### Do skirting vents have to go on all four sides of the home?

Not all four, but HUD's rule requires openings on at least two opposite sides so air can cross the crawl space instead of just sitting there. It's better to spread vents around the full perimeter, weighted toward opposite sides and the corners where airflow stalls first. A home vented on only one side can hit the total square footage and still not move air well.

### Should I close my skirting vents in the winter in Florida?

Generally no. Central Florida cold snaps are short, and closed vents trap the same humid air they're supposed to move out. If you have adjustable covers and a hard freeze is forecast, closing them for those nights and opening them again afterward is reasonable. The rest of the year, keep them open, screened, and clear.

### Does a vapor barrier mean I can remove my skirting vents?

No, a full ground vapor barrier lowers the legal minimum vent area, it doesn't eliminate it. Most crawl space moisture comes from the ground, so covering it cuts what the vents carry, but outside humidity and moisture from plumbing or AC condensation still need somewhere to go. Removing vents below the reduced minimum isn't compliant either way.
