If the seam down the middle of your double-wide is opening up, the two halves are usually sitting at different heights or have shifted against each other. The fix starts under the home: level both sections, then re-fasten and patch what the movement damaged. Patch the seam first and it will open again.

This guide covers what the marriage line mobile home owners hear about actually is, the signs that it is separating, what causes it in Marion County, and why the order of the repair matters. We level homes. We do not do roofing or drywall, and we will say so where it matters.

## What the Marriage Line Is

A double-wide is two boxes built separately, hauled to the lot, and joined down the middle. The joint is the marriage line. Installers and HUD's rules call it the mate line. Same thing.

At that joint the two sections are fastened together at the floor, the walls, and the roof. Under the home, a row of piers carries the center of each section. Up top, the roof halves meet at the ridge, and a cap or closure covers the seam. Inside, trim or a strip hides the joint in the ceiling and sometimes the walls.

Every one of those connections assumes the two halves stay at the same height. When they don't, the seam shows it first.

## Signs the Marriage Line Is Separating

You will see some of these inside, some outside, and some only from under the home.

- **A crack or gap running along the ceiling down the center of the home.** It often follows the ridge line from one end to the other, or shows up in one room and stops.
- **A gap or lip at the floor seam.** One side of the floor sits higher than the other, or the flooring or carpet seam has pulled open or humped up.
- **Doors near the center of the home sticking.** Hallway and pass-through doors that used to swing freely start to rub or won't latch.
- **A roof cap or seam that leaks.** Water stains on the ceiling along the center line after a heavy summer rain point to the roof seam. Settling can open it, but a failed seal can too. A roofer has to look at that part.
- **Cabinets or trim pulling away where the halves meet.** Check the kitchen and any wall that crosses the center line.
- **Daylight, drafts, or bugs coming in at the seam.** If you can see light through the joint, the home has moved a lot.

One crack alone is not a diagnosis. Two or three of these together, especially along the center line, is the pattern to take seriously. Our post on [cracks in mobile home walls](/blog/cracks-mobile-home-walls/) covers how to tell cosmetic cracks from settling cracks in general. The marriage line is the double-wide-specific version.

## Why the Two Halves Drift Apart

The seam does not open by itself. Something under the home lets one section move relative to the other. These are the usual causes.

**Center-line piers settling.** The marriage line piers carry a share of the roof and floor load for both halves. They also sit where water tends to collect and where the crawl space is hardest to reach and check. Sandy Marion County soil can wash out or compress under a pad after a wet summer, and a pier that sinks a half inch pulls its section down with it.

**One section settling more than the other.** If the north half sits on well-packed ground and the south half sits where the downspouts drain, they will not settle at the same rate. The difference between them is what strains the seam.

**Loose or failed interconnection fasteners.** The sections are tied together with fasteners along the joint. Years of movement, and in older homes some corrosion, can loosen them. Once they let go, the halves shift independently.

**A past DIY or bad lift.** Lifting one half of a double-wide without the other can open the marriage line on the spot. If a previous owner tried to level the home that way, the seam may have been opened long before you bought it.

**Wind and storm movement.** Hurricane-season wind loads push on both sections. A home with weak anchoring or loose fasteners moves more.

## What HUD's Installation Rules Say

The federal installation standards for manufactured homes cover this joint. Here is what we verified in the current text of 24 CFR Part 3285.

[24 CFR 3285.802](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.802) covers structural interconnection of multi-section homes. It says that structural interconnections along the interior and exterior at the mate-line are necessary to join all sections of the home. It also says that after the exterior close-up is complete, no gaps are permitted between the structural elements being interconnected along the mate-line.

[24 CFR 3285.310](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.310) covers pier location and spacing. It says mate-line and column pier supports must follow that subpart and its figures, unless a registered professional engineer or architect designs a different support and footing setup.

Read those as installation standards, not repair specs. They tell you the sections are meant to be joined tight and supported under the mate line. They do not tell you what to do about a gap that opened years later. That is where the level comes in.

## Why the Fix Starts With Leveling Both Halves

You can fill a ceiling gap, caulk a floor seam, and reseal a roof cap. If the halves are still at different heights, the seam moves again. The patch cracks, the caulk tears, and the leak comes back.

So the order is:

1. **Measure the whole home.** Both sections, the center-line piers, and the perimeter piers. The goal is to find out which piers dropped and by how much.
2. **Level both halves, together.** The crew lifts in small steps and alternates between the two sections so the seam closes evenly. Lifting one side alone is how a gap gets worse.
3. **Re-set the center-line piers.** Replace anything cracked, crushed, or leaning, and make sure each pier sits on a solid pad.
4. **Check the interconnection.** With the halves back at the same height, any fasteners or ties across the joint that came loose can be re-secured.
5. **Then patch.** Ceiling seams, roof cap, trim, and paint come last, after the home has held its position.

HUD's final-leveling rule is the benchmark for step 2. [24 CFR 3285.6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.6) says a home is considered adequately leveled if there is no more than 1/4 inch difference between adjacent pier supports (frame or perimeter) and the exterior doors and windows do not bind and can be properly operated.

## What You Can Check Yourself

You can learn a lot from above the floor.

- Walk the center line and look for a seam that is higher on one side. Run your hand across it.
- Open and close every door near the middle of the home. Note which ones rub and where.
- Look up at the ceiling seam in good light. Note where the gap is widest. That is usually near the piers that dropped most.
- Take photos with a coin or tape measure next to the gap. Come back in a month and compare.

Staying out from under the home is part of the job. If you do crawl under to look, never get under a home supported only by a jack, and never put any part of your body under a load that a jack is holding. Jacks slip. Leave lifting to a crew with proper support.
