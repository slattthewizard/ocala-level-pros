## How Florida Wind Zones Work for Mobile Homes

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) sets the construction standards for every manufactured home built in this country. After Andrew, HUD overhauled those standards, and the new wind rules took effect on July 13, 1994. Since then, every manufactured home has been built for one of three wind zones:

- **Wind Zone 1** covers most of the interior United States. Homes are designed for winds up to about 70 mph (fastest mile).
- **Wind Zone 2** covers areas with higher hurricane exposure, including most of inland Florida. Homes are designed for winds up to about 100 mph.
- **Wind Zone 3** covers the highest-risk coastal areas, including South Florida and strips of coastline within a mile of the water in some Gulf counties. Homes are designed for winds up to about 110 mph.

Those numbers use an older "fastest mile" measurement, so they don't line up exactly with the sustained wind speeds you hear on the news. The practical takeaway is simpler: a Zone 2 home has stronger roof connections, more fasteners, tougher siding attachment, and more tie-down points built into its frame than a Zone 1 home.

One more rule matters a lot in Florida. A home can always be installed in a zone equal to or lower than the one it was built for, but never higher. A Zone 3 home can sit in Marion County with no problem. A Zone 1 home cannot legally be installed anywhere in Florida, because the entire state is Zone 2 or Zone 3. You can read HUD's own explanation of the program at [hud.gov](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/rmra/mhs).

## Marion County Is Wind Zone 2: Here's What That Means for You

Marion County, along with Sumter County and most of Citrus County, falls in Wind Zone 2. We're inland enough to miss the worst coastal surge and the strongest landfall winds, but close enough to both coasts that hurricanes still reach us with plenty of punch left.

For homeowners, Zone 2 status means four practical things.

**First, your home's construction matters.** If your home was built after July 13, 1994, it should carry a Zone 2 or Zone 3 rating and was engineered for those wind loads at the factory. If it was built between 1976 and mid-1994, it was built to the older, weaker HUD standard. It's still legal to live in and keep where it sits, but it wasn't designed for the same winds.

**Second, your installation matters just as much.** The strongest home in the world won't perform if it's sitting on undersized piers with rusted straps. Florida's installation standards, enforced by the Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles through licensed installers, spell out anchor spacing, strap requirements, and pier construction for our zone. You can find the state's mobile home resources at [flhsmv.gov](https://www.flhsmv.gov/).

**Third, insurance companies care.** Carriers writing policies in Florida routinely ask for the wind zone, the anchoring condition, and sometimes a tie-down certification before they'll bind or renew coverage.

**Fourth, buyers and inspectors care.** Wind zone and tie-down condition come up in nearly every pre-purchase inspection we see in Marion County.

## How to Find Your Mobile Home's Wind Zone

You don't need to guess, and you don't need to pay anyone to tell you. Your home's wind zone is printed on its **data plate**, a paper label about the size of a sheet of notebook paper that the factory attached inside the home. Common spots:

- Inside a kitchen cabinet door
- Inside the master bedroom closet
- Near the electrical panel
- Under the kitchen sink

The data plate includes a small U.S. map showing the wind zone (and roof load and thermal zones) the home was built for. It also lists the manufacture date, which tells you instantly whether the home falls under the post-1994 standards.

There's also a red metal **HUD certification label** on the outside of each section of the home, but that tag only proves the home met HUD standards. It doesn't state the wind zone. For that, you need the data plate.

If the data plate is missing, painted over, or unreadable, you can request the information from the Institute for Building Technology and Safety using the home's serial number, which is usually stamped on the frame near the hitch. When buyers in Dunnellon, the Parkers, called us about a used double-wide priced $9,000 under market, a quick data plate check explained the discount. The home was a Zone 1 unit trucked down from Tennessee, which meant it couldn't legally be re-installed in Florida. That ten-minute check saved them from a purchase they couldn't have sited anywhere in the state.

## Wind Zone 2 vs. Zone 1 and Zone 3: Side by Side

Here's how the three zones compare, and what each one means for a home in Marion County.

| | Wind Zone 1 | Wind Zone 2 (Marion County) | Wind Zone 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design wind speed (fastest mile) | About 70 mph | About 100 mph | About 110 mph |
| Where it applies | Most inland U.S. states | Most of inland Florida, including Marion, Sumter, and most of Citrus County | South Florida and coastal strips within about a mile of the Gulf in some counties |
| Can it be installed in Marion County? | No, not legal anywhere in Florida | Yes | Yes |
| Typical tie-down demand | Fewer anchors, wider spacing | More anchors, closer spacing, frame ties required | Most anchors, closest spacing, often added longitudinal protection |
| Typical anchoring upgrade cost here | Not applicable | $1,500 to $3,500 for a full single-wide system | $2,000 to $4,500 for a full system |

A couple of notes on that table. Anchor counts and spacing depend on your home's length, width, roof style, and the soil it sits on, so two identical homes two streets apart can need different layouts. ## What Zone 2 Means for Insurance, Inspections, and Closings

This is where wind zones stop being trivia and start costing or saving you money.

Florida insurers have tightened up hard on manufactured homes over the past several years. When you apply for or renew a policy, the carrier typically wants to know three things: the year the home was built, the wind zone on the data plate, and the condition of the anchoring system. Homes built to the post-1994 Zone 2 or Zone 3 standard are simply easier and cheaper to insure. Pre-1994 homes can usually still get coverage, but carriers lean harder on proof that the tie-downs are present, tight, and rust-free.

## Keeping a Zone 2 Home Ready: What You Can Check and When to Call a Pro

You don't need special tools to do a basic once-a-year check on your own home. Here's what's safe to do yourself:

- **Read your data plate** and write down the wind zone and build date somewhere you'll find them.
- **Walk the perimeter** and look at every visible strap. Surface rust is common; flaking, pitted, or broken straps are not.
- **Check strap tension** by pressing on them. A healthy strap feels tight, like a guitar string. A loose one flexes easily.
- **Look at the ground around anchor heads.** Soil that's washed out or heaved around an anchor means it may not hold rated load anymore.
- **Watch the home itself for movement clues.** Doors that suddenly stick, new gaps at the skirting, or a floor that slopes can mean the piers under your home have shifted, which puts extra stress on every strap.

Here's where the line is. Checking is homeowner work. Fixing is not. Anchors have to be driven to a specific depth, matched to your soil type, and tensioned correctly, and straps under load can injure you badly if they let go while you're working on them. Jacking any part of the home to correct piers is absolutely professional territory. If your walkaround turns up problems, stop there and bring in someone licensed.

Anchoring and leveling also go together more than most owners realize. Straps anchor the frame, but the frame rests on piers, and when piers settle the whole system loosens. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What wind zone is Marion County, Florida for mobile homes?

Marion County is in HUD Wind Zone 2, along with Sumter County and most of Citrus County. Homes built after July 13, 1994 for Zone 2 are designed for winds of about 100 mph on the old fastest-mile scale. Zone 3 homes are also fine to install here, since a home can always be placed in an equal or lower zone.

### Can I put a Wind Zone 1 mobile home in Florida?

No. Every county in Florida is designated Zone 2 or Zone 3, and federal and state rules prohibit installing a home in a higher wind zone than it was built for. A Zone 1 home already legally sited in Florida before the rules changed can stay, but it can't be relocated within the state. Always check the data plate before buying a used home from out of state.

### My mobile home was built before 1994. Do I have to upgrade it?

You're not required to rebuild the home itself, and it's grandfathered where it sits. But your anchoring system still has to be maintained, and insurers, lenders, and buyers will hold it to modern expectations. Upgrading straps and anchors on an older home typically costs $1,500 to $3,500 and is often the difference between smooth coverage and a non-renewal notice.

### How do I find my mobile home's wind zone rating?

Look for the paper data plate inside the home, usually in a kitchen cabinet, the master bedroom closet, or near the electrical panel. It shows a small map with the wind zone the home was built for, plus the manufacture date. If the plate is missing, the information can be requested using the serial number stamped on the frame, or a professional inspection can document what's actually installed.
