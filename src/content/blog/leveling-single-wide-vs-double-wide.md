Here's a number that surprises most homeowners: a double wide usually sits on twice as many piers as a single wide, sometimes more. That one fact drives almost everything about how the two homes get leveled, how long the job takes, and what it costs.

If you own a double wide in Marion County and your doors have started sticking, you might be tempted to price the job like your neighbor's single wide. Don't. Double wide leveling is a different animal, and knowing why will save you from lowball quotes that fall apart and high quotes you can't check. This guide walks through exactly what changes between the two, with real numbers from jobs around Ocala.

## The Frame Is the First Big Difference

A single wide is one box on one steel frame. Two main I-beams run the length of the home, and piers sit under those beams every 6 to 8 feet. When a single wide settles, the fix is usually straightforward: jack the low beam, adjust or shim the piers, and check the level as you go.

A double wide is two boxes built separately, hauled to your lot separately, and joined down the middle. That joint is called the marriage line, and it's the spine of the whole house. A typical double wide has four main I-beams (two per section) plus a row of piers directly under the marriage line supporting the ridge beam, heavy walls, and any openings between the two halves.

So where a 70-foot single wide might have 18 to 24 piers, a similar-length double wide often carries 40 to 50 or more. Every one of those piers is a spot that can settle, and every one has to be checked during a relevel.

The marriage line changes the physics too. When one half of a double wide drops even half an inch relative to the other, the two sections start pulling against each other. You'll see it as a gap opening in the ceiling down the centerline, cracks in the drywall where the halves meet, or a hump or valley in the floor along the middle of the house. A single wide can't develop any of those problems because there's no seam to separate.

## What Double Wide Leveling Involves That a Single Wide Doesn't

Beyond pier count, four things make double wide leveling a more careful job.

**The two halves have to move together.** You can't just jack up the low corner of one section until it reads level. Lift one half too far or too fast and you stress the marriage line fasteners, crack the ceiling closure strip, and sometimes split the roof cap. A good crew lifts in small increments, alternating between sections, checking the marriage line gap constantly.

**The centerline piers do the heavy lifting.** Marriage line piers carry point loads from columns and headers, especially around big openings like a pass-through kitchen or a wide living room. These piers settle more often than perimeter piers because they carry more weight. They're also the hardest to reach, dead center under the house.

**There's more that can hide the problem.** A double wide has more floor area, more ductwork, and more plumbing runs underneath. Crews spend more time crawling, and there's more belly board and insulation in the way.

**Utilities cross the marriage line.** Ducts, wiring, and sometimes plumbing cross from one section to the other. If the halves have shifted, those crossover connections may have pulled loose. A proper double wide relevel includes checking them, because a separated duct under the house can quietly double your power bill.

## How the Work Actually Goes, Step by Step

The core process is the same for both home types, and we walk through it in detail in [how mobile home leveling works](/blog/how-mobile-home-leveling-works/). Here's the short version, with the double wide differences flagged.

**Step 1: Reading the home.** The crew checks floor level inside with a water level or laser, noting every low spot. On a double wide, they also measure the marriage line gap at several points along the ceiling and check whether the two sections read level relative to each other, not just to the ground.

**Step 2: Underside inspection.** Every pier gets looked at: cracked blocks, tilted stacks, rotted wood caps, shims that have crushed or slipped. On a double wide, the centerline row gets extra attention because it fails first. This is also when the crew checks your anchors and straps, since a jack can't safely lift near a strap that's about to snap. If your straps look rusty, that's a separate fix covered on our [tie-down service page](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/).

**Step 3: Lifting.** Hydraulic jacks go under the I-beams, never under the floor itself. A single wide gets lifted beam by beam. A double wide gets lifted in an alternating pattern, a little on one section, a little on the other, so the marriage line closes evenly instead of binding.

**Step 4: Shimming and resetting.** Hardwood or steel shims go between pier and beam at each corrected point. Tilted piers get restacked. HUD's installation standards, spelled out in [24 CFR Part 3285](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/part-3285), set the requirements for pier spacing and load capacity that installers follow, and a relevel should leave your setup meeting them.

**Step 5: Verification.** Level readings get taken again inside, doors get tested, and on a double wide the marriage line gap gets measured one more time. If a door still rubs after the frame reads level, that's usually a door problem now, not a foundation problem. ## Florida Soil Doesn't Care Which Home You Own, But It Punishes Double Wides More

Central Florida sits on sandy soil over limestone, and that sand moves. Heavy summer rain saturates it, dry spells shrink it, and piers ride up and down with it a little every year. The Florida Division of Emergency Management notes that proper installation and anchoring are the biggest factors in how manufactured homes handle severe weather, and a level frame is where proper anchoring starts.

Both home types settle here. The difference is tolerance. A single wide that's a half inch out of level is annoying. A double wide that's a half inch out along the marriage line is actively damaging itself: the seam works open, fasteners loosen, and the roof cap flexes. That's why we suggest double wide owners check level every year or two, while single wide owners can often stretch to three, unless something obvious shows up sooner.

There's an insurance angle too. During a 4-point inspection or a tie-down certification for a Florida policy, an inspector who sees a wavy roofline or a gapped marriage line will flag it, and some carriers won't bind coverage until it's corrected. If you're buying or selling a double wide in Marion County, get the level checked before the inspector does. It's a cheap problem to fix early and an expensive one to discover at closing.

## Where DIY Ends for Each Home Type

We're straight with people about this. Some leveling-adjacent work is fine to do yourself, and some isn't.

For a single wide, a capable owner with a proper bottle jack rated well above the load, solid cribbing, and a lot of patience can sometimes re-shim one or two accessible piers. Even then, one mistake matters: never get under a home supported only by a jack, and never lift more than the small amount needed to slip a shim.

For a double wide, our honest advice is don't. Lifting one section without coordinating the other is exactly how the Silver Springs home ended up with a $3,800 repair instead of a $1,300 one. The marriage line makes staged, measured lifting mandatory, and that takes multiple jacks, two sets of hands, and experience reading how the halves respond. The money you'd save doing it yourself is small next to what a split roof cap or torn ceiling costs.

Either way, stop and call a professional if you see any of these: piers leaning more than a few degrees, cracked or crumbling blocks, a marriage line gap you can see daylight through, soft or sunken soil under multiple piers, or a home that's settled noticeably after one heavy rain. Those point to problems a shim won't fix.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a double wide cost twice as much to level as a single wide?

Usually a bit more than twice, on average. Most single wide relevels in the Ocala area run $450 to $900, while double wides run $900 to $2,000. The gap comes from pier count and crew time: a double wide has roughly twice the piers plus a marriage line that requires slow, staged lifting.

### How do I know if my double wide's marriage line has separated?

Check the ceiling along the centerline of the house for a gap, a crack, or a strip of trim that's pulled loose. Other signs include a hump or dip in the floor down the middle, drywall cracks where the two halves meet, and interior doors near the centerline that suddenly stick. Any visible ceiling gap is worth a professional look soon, since water and energy loss follow separation.

### How often should a double wide be releveled in Florida?

Have the level checked every year or two, and expect to actually relevel every 3 to 5 years in Central Florida's sandy soil. Homes on well-compacted pads with good drainage go longer, and homes with water pooling underneath go shorter. Checking is cheap, and catching settlement early keeps the fix in the hundreds instead of the thousands.

### Can I level just one side of my double wide?

No, and this is the most common DIY mistake we see. The two sections are fastened together at the floor, walls, and roof, so lifting one side changes the load on the other and can open the marriage line or crack the roof cap. Both halves need to be measured and lifted together in small, alternating stages, which is why double wide leveling is a two-tech job.
