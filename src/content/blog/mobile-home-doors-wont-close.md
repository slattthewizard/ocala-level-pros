## What a Mobile Home Door That Won't Close Is Really Telling You

Your doors are the most sensitive level indicators in your house. A door only closes cleanly when its frame is a true rectangle. Shift one corner of that frame by a quarter inch and the door starts rubbing. Shift it by half an inch and it won't latch at all.

Site-built homes sit on continuous foundations, so they move as one unit. Mobile homes are different. Your home rests on a steel frame supported by a series of piers, usually concrete blocks on pads, spaced every 6 to 8 feet. Each pier settles independently. When one pier sinks even slightly, the frame above it flexes, the walls attached to that frame rack out of square, and every door and window in that section goes crooked with them.

That's why sticking doors in a mobile home tend to show up in clusters. One door acting up might be humidity or a loose hinge. Two or three doors acting up in the same end of the home is a settling pattern, and it's worth taking seriously.

## The Chain Reaction: From Florida Soil to Your Door Frame

Here's the sequence that ends with a door that won't close, step by step:

1. **The soil moves first.** Central Florida sits on sandy soil over limestone. Sand compacts under load, and our summer rain cycle makes it worse. Soil swells when saturated, then shrinks as it dries, and piers ride that movement up and down. If you want the geology version, the [USGS explains why Florida karst terrain is so prone to subsidence](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-sinkhole).
2. **A pier loses full contact.** Once soil settles under a pad, the pier either sinks with it or ends up carrying less than its share. Nearby piers pick up the extra weight and start compressing their own soil faster.
3. **The steel frame deflects.** The I-beams under your home are strong, but they're designed to be supported every few feet. Take away one support and the beam sags between its neighbors.
4. **The walls rack.** Your wall panels are attached to the floor system. When the floor tilts, rectangular door openings lean into parallelograms. The hinge side and latch side are no longer plumb with each other.
5. **The door tells on everything.** It rubs at the top corner, scrapes the threshold, or the latch misses the strike plate entirely.

Notice the door is the last step in the chain, not the first. That's why fixing the door without fixing the piers never holds. It's also why sticking doors often travel with other symptoms: gaps over interior walls, hairline cracks near door corners, and floors that feel slightly downhill toward one end.

## Quick Checks You Can Do This Weekend

Before you call anyone, spend 20 minutes ruling out the simple stuff. You'll either save yourself a service call or walk into the conversation knowing exactly what you're dealing with.

**Check the hinges first.** Open the door and lift up on the handle. If the whole door moves noticeably, your hinge screws are loose or stripped. Tighten them, or replace the top hinge screws with 3-inch screws that bite into the framing. That fix costs about $2.

**Look at the reveal.** Close the door as far as it goes and study the gap between the door and frame. An even gap all the way around that's just tight means swelling, common in our humid summers. A gap that's wide at one corner and pinched at the opposite corner means the frame is out of square. That's a settling signal, not a door problem.

**Do the marble test.** Set a marble or golf ball on the floor in the hallway and in the room with the sticking door. If it rolls the same direction every time, your floor has a slope.

**Count your doors.** Walk the whole home and test every interior and exterior door. One bad door could be anything. Multiple bad doors on the same end or side of the home is a pattern.

**Check the season.** Write down which doors stick and check them again after a dry week. Doors that stick only in August but close fine in January are reacting to soil moisture and humidity swings. That still points to marginal pier support. A properly supported home shouldn't change that much between seasons.

**What not to do:** don't crawl under the home and start adjusting piers or stacking shims yourself. A single-wide can weigh 15,000 pounds or more, and a pier that shifts while you're under there is a genuine, life-threatening hazard. Checking from inside the home is smart homework. Working under the frame is a job for people with the jacks, cribbing, and training to do it safely.

Shaving, adjusting strike plates, and bending latch tongues all treat the symptom while the cause keeps working underneath you. Meanwhile, the same settling that's racking your door frames is doing quieter damage:

- Stressing plumbing joints under the floor, which leads to slow leaks and standing water under the home
- Flexing the subfloor until seams open up and you get soft spots in the floor
- Pulling siding and skirting out of alignment
- Opening gaps around exterior doors that let in moisture, bugs, and hot air

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do my mobile home doors stick in summer but close fine in winter?

That pattern usually means two things are stacking up: wood and weatherstripping swell in humid months, and Florida's soil expands and contracts with the rain cycle, which moves marginally supported piers. A well-supported home shouldn't change enough seasonally for doors to stop latching. If the swing between seasons is that dramatic, have the piers and shims checked, because seasonal sticking is often the early stage of year-round sticking.

### Can I just adjust or replace the door instead of releveling?

You can, but if settling is the cause, the new door will start sticking within months because the opening itself is out of square and still moving. You'll have spent $150 to $400 on a door that solves nothing. The reliable order of operations is to level the home first, then see if the door needs any work at all. In most of our jobs, doors close normally the moment the frame comes back to level.

### How fast does a sticking door turn into expensive damage?

There's no fixed timeline, but in our experience around Ocala, homes that ignore multiple sticking doors for two or more years often graduate to soft floors, plumbing leaks, or cracked piers, and those repairs run $1,200 to $5,000 instead of $400 to $900 for a relevel. Florida's wet season accelerates everything because saturated soil settles faster. The cheapest version of this repair is always the earliest one.

### Is a mobile home door that won't close a sign of a sinkhole?

Almost never. Marion County does sit on karst limestone, but the overwhelming majority of sticking doors trace back to ordinary pier settling, soil compaction, or moisture swings. Sinkhole warning signs look different: rapidly widening cracks, doors that go from fine to jammed in days, depressions forming in the yard. If you're seeing that combination, get a professional out quickly. Otherwise, assume settling, because that's what it is more than 95 percent of the time.
