## What an FHA Foundation Certification Actually Is

When you finance a manufactured home with an FHA loan, HUD requires proof that the home sits on a permanent foundation. The proof is a certification letter from a licensed professional engineer (or in some cases a registered architect) who inspects the home in person or reviews detailed photos and documentation from a trained inspector.

The engineer compares what's under your home against the [HUD Permanent Foundations Guide for Manufactured Housing](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/publications/destech/permfound.html), usually shortened to PFGMH. If everything checks out, you get a signed, sealed letter. Your lender sends it to the underwriter, and that condition comes off your file.

A few things the certification is not:

- It's not an appraisal. The appraiser flags that the home is manufactured, which triggers the requirement. The engineer handles the actual certification.
- It's not a four point inspection. Insurance companies want that one. We covered it in our post on [four point inspections for mobile homes](/blog/four-point-inspection-mobile-home/).
- It's not optional. Underwriters can't waive it on a manufactured home, no matter how solid the place looks.

The letter typically costs $400 to $650 in our area and takes 3 to 7 business days once the inspection happens. The catch is that a lot of homes don't pass on the first look, and that's where the timeline gets tight.

## HUD Permanent Foundation Requirements Your Mobile Home Must Meet

The PFGMH runs a few hundred pages, but for a typical single wide or double wide in Central Florida, the engineer is checking four big things.

**1. The home must bear on a permanent support system.** Piers under the steel frame need to sit on footings that transfer the load to the ground. Dry stacked blocks on bare dirt won't cut it. The engineer wants to see poured footings, ABS pads rated for the load, or runners, with piers built and spaced correctly. If you want the deeper background, our post on [permanent foundations versus piers](/blog/permanent-foundation-vs-piers/) walks through the difference.

**2. The home must be anchored against wind and flotation.** In Florida that means tie downs and ground anchors that meet state wind zone requirements. Marion County sits in Wind Zone II, so spacing and strap condition matter. Rusted straps and loose anchor heads are the single most common failure we see. Our [mobile home tie down service in Ocala](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/) exists mostly because of this requirement.

**3. The wheels, axles, and tongue must be removed.** HUD's logic is simple: a permanent home shouldn't be ready to roll away. Plenty of older homes in Silver Springs and Citra still have axles bolted on under the belly. Removing them is usually a quick job, but the engineer will fail the certification if they're still attached.

**4. The perimeter must be enclosed and the site drained.** Skirting or a perimeter wall has to enclose the crawl space, with proper ventilation and access. The ground under the home should be graded so water runs away from it, and in our climate a vapor barrier over the soil is expected. Standing water under the home is a red flag for both the engineer and the appraiser.

There's a fifth item people forget: documentation. The engineer wants the HUD data plate and certification labels (the little red tags) present and legible. Homes built before June 15, 1976 don't have them and generally can't get FHA financing at all.

## What the Engineer Checks During an FHA Foundation Certification Inspection

Here's how the inspection actually goes, so you know what the engineer or their field inspector will do at the property.

They'll open the skirting access panel and get under the home with a flashlight and camera. Expect 45 minutes to an hour and a half underneath. They photograph pier construction, footing type, strap connections, anchor heads, the belly board, and any signs of movement or water.

They'll check pier spacing against the home's data plate and setup requirements, look for shimming that's crushed or slipped, and tug on straps to test tension. They'll note whether the frame is level, because a home that's out of level usually means piers have settled, and settled piers mean the support system isn't performing.

Then they'll walk the perimeter, checking skirting condition, ventilation, grading, and whether the tongue and axles are gone. Inside, they may note doors that won't latch or floors that slope, since those symptoms point back at the foundation.

## Common Reasons Mobile Homes Fail the First Inspection

After years of retrofit calls, we see the same failures over and over. In rough order of frequency around Marion County:

1. **Dry stacked piers with no footings.** Blocks sitting on dirt, sometimes on a scrap of plywood. This is the number one killer, especially on homes set before the county tightened enforcement.
2. **Rusted or missing tie down straps.** Our sandy, humid soil eats galvanized steel. If your straps are original to a 1990s setup, assume some have failed.
3. **Axles and tongue still attached.** Free to fail, cheap to fix.
4. **Standing water or missing vapor barrier.** Water under the home suggests grading problems, and engineers won't sign off on a crawl space that stays wet.
5. **Home out of level.** Settled piers show up as gaps between the frame and pier caps, crushed shims, and doors that stick inside.
6. **Skirting gaps or missing access.** Minor, but it can hold up the letter.

None of these are exotic. Every one of them is a routine repair for a crew that works under manufactured homes daily. The mistake we see buyers make is hiring a general handyman who's never read the PFGMH, then failing the re-inspection because the piers were rebuilt without footings. The engineer certifies to HUD's standard, not to "looks sturdy."

One honest caution about DIY: you can clear brush, fix skirting panels, and improve grading yourself. Do not jack the home, rebuild piers, or tension straps on your own. A double wide can weigh 40,000 pounds, and a slipped jack under a steel frame is how people get killed. When the job involves lifting or load bearing supports, stop and call a professional.

**Order the certification early.** Don't wait for the underwriter to ask. If the appraisal says "manufactured home," the requirement is coming. Ordering the engineer letter the same week you go under contract buys you time to handle surprises.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long is an FHA foundation certification good for?

The engineer's letter certifies the foundation as of the inspection date, and lenders generally want one dated within the current transaction. If your loan falls through and you go under contract again months later, the new lender will usually accept the existing letter if nothing has changed, but many ask for a fresh one after 6 to 12 months. Keep your copy either way, since it's useful for the next sale.

### Can I get an FHA loan on a mobile home built before 1976?

No. Homes built before June 15, 1976 predate the HUD code and don't carry the required certification labels, so they're not eligible for FHA financing regardless of foundation condition. Buyers of pre-1976 homes typically use cash or chattel financing. The foundation still matters for safety and insurance, just not for an FHA letter.

### Who hires the engineer, the buyer or the seller?

Usually the buyer or their lender orders the certification, since it's a loan condition, and the buyer pays the $400 to $650 fee. Repair costs are negotiable between the parties. Sellers who pre-inspect and retrofit before listing often net more, because FHA and VA buyers can bid without worrying the deal will die under the home.

### What happens if the home fails the certification?

You get a report listing the deficiencies, you fix them, and the engineer recertifies, often from contractor photos for a reduced fee. Typical retrofits in our area run $1,500 to $6,000 depending on home size and pier count. Most fixes take one to two days of site work, so a failed first inspection doesn't have to blow your closing date if you move quickly.
