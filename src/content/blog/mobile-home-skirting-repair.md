Most mobile home skirting repair is a panel swap or a short run of new track, and it costs a fraction of a new install. Replace the whole skirt only when the damage runs along most of the home, when the vinyl has gone brittle everywhere, or when the home itself is moving. On our price sheet, skirting repairs run $250-$800 and a full install runs $1,200-$3,500.

The hard part is telling which one you have. This guide sorts the damage we see in Marion County into three piles: swap a panel, fix the track or trim, or start over. It also covers how to replace a single vinyl panel yourself, and the one warning sign that the skirting is not the problem at all.

## The Quick Sort: Panel, Track, or Whole Run

Walk the full perimeter before you decide anything. A skirting problem that looks like one bad corner often turns out to be four small ones, and four small ones changes the answer.

Here is how the common failures break down:

- One or two cracked or blown-out panels, rest tight: panel swap.
- Panels pop out at the bottom in the same spot every time: the bottom track is bent, buried, or loose. Fix the track, not the panel.
- Top trim pulled away from the home along one wall: re-fasten or replace that length of trim.
- A hole chewed or pushed through a panel: patch or swap the panel, then deal with whatever made it. More on that below.
- Panels brittle, chalky, and cracking across most of the home: the material has aged out. Replace the run.
- Panels bowed, pushed out, or gapped at the corners along a whole side: this may be a settling home, not a skirting failure. Read the leveling section before you spend money.

As a rule of thumb, if you are replacing more than roughly half the panels anyway, the new track, trim, and labor on the rest make a full install the better buy. That is a judgment call, and we will give you our read when we look at it.

## Storm Damage and Blown-Out Panels

Wind gets under a loose panel edge and pulls the panel out of its track. After a hard storm you will often find a few panels on the ground, sometimes across the yard, with the rest of the skirt still standing.

If the panels are intact and the track is straight, you can often just slide them back in. If they are cracked, they need to be swapped. Check the screws or fasteners along the top, too. A panel that blew out once usually had a loose top edge before the storm.

Look at the wall that took the hit. Wind-driven rain pushes water behind skirting, and a wall that lost panels may have lost some ground cover under the home as well. Check underneath before you close the skirt back up.

## Cracked, Faded, and Brittle Vinyl

Florida sun is hard on vinyl, and panels on the walls that take the most afternoon sun fade and go brittle first. A brittle panel cracks along the vent slots, at the corners, and anywhere a screw sits too tight.

A few cracks on the sunny side are a repair. You can swap those panels and the rest of the skirt keeps going. The thing to test is the panels that look fine. Press a thumb against the face of a panel on the sunny wall. If it flexes and springs back, it has life left. If it snaps, clicks, or leaves a crack line, replacing a panel at a time will turn into a yearly chore.

A color match is the other catch. New panels on an old skirt rarely match exactly, because the old ones have faded. If your park has color rules, check with management before you order.

## Bent Track, Buried Track, and Loose Top Trim

When panels keep popping loose in the same stretch, the track is the cause, and replacing panels will not hold.

Bottom track, buried or bent. Marion County sand shifts and washes. Soil piles up against the bottom track after heavy rain, or washes out from under it and leaves the track hanging and bent. A buried track traps water against the panels. A bent track will not hold the panel bottom in place. Dig the soil back, straighten or replace that length of track, and regrade so water runs away from the home.

Top trim, pulled loose. The top trim or rail is what holds the panels from above. If it has pulled away from the home, the panels hang crooked and the top edge rattles. Re-fasten it into solid framing, not just the siding, and replace any length that has cracked.

Federal installation rules say skirting must not be attached in a way that traps water between the siding and trim, and must not block the home's siding from expanding and contracting ([24 CFR 3285.504](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.504)). Both matter on a repair. A top rail screwed down tight and sealed along its whole length breaks both rules.

## Animal Entry Holes

Raccoons, armadillos, and stray cats push through weak panels and vent screens. A small hole in a panel is a patch or a panel swap. The hole is not the real problem, though. If something got in once, it will get in again and may already be living under the home.

Before you replace the panel, look under there. Our guide to [animals under a mobile home](/blog/animals-under-mobile-home/) covers the signs, what damage to look for in the belly and insulation, and how to close the entry without trapping an animal inside. Then fix the skirting.

Do not close up the vents to stop animals. Vents have to stay, covered with a rodent-proof screen. The federal rule for crawlspace ventilation is in [24 CFR 3285.505](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.505), and our post on [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/) explains how much vent area your home needs.

## Mower and Trimmer Damage

String trimmers chew the bottom edge of vinyl panels and mowers throw rocks through them. Chips and scuffs at the bottom of a panel are cosmetic, and you can leave them. A cracked corner or a split along the face is a panel swap.

If the same stretch gets hit every time, the cheap fix is a few inches of gravel or edging along the base so the trimmer has somewhere else to go. It also keeps soil from piling against the track.

## How to Replace a Single Vinyl Panel

The steps below fit the common snap-in vinyl systems. Panel systems differ by brand, so check how yours locks together before you pull anything.

1. **Find the panel and its neighbors.** Look at how the panels overlap and which direction they slide. Note where the vent panels and the access panel sit.
2. **Open the top trim.** Pop the top trim or release the top channel on the panel and the one beside it. Do not force it. Forcing cracks good panels.
3. **Lift the panel out of the bottom track.** Slide the damaged panel sideways out of its neighbors, or lift it up and out of the bottom track, depending on your system.
4. **Check what is behind it.** This is your chance to look at the track, the ground, and the screens. Fix a bent track now, before the new panel goes in.
5. **Cut the new panel to height.** Measure the gap from the top trim to the bottom track on this spot, because the ground is rarely flat. Mark the new panel, score it with a utility knife and snap it, or cut it with a fine-tooth blade. Leave the manufacturer's expansion gap at the top and bottom. Vinyl grows in Florida heat, and a panel jammed in tight on a cool morning can bow by afternoon.
6. **Seat the new panel.** Set the bottom into the track first, slide it into its neighbors, then tuck the top into the trim or channel.
7. **Fasten and check.** Fasten only where the system calls for it, and do not drive screws tight. Run your hand along the face. The new panel should sit flush with the ones beside it and move slightly when pushed.

If the replacement is a vent or access panel, use the same style so the vent area and the access opening stay what the home needs.

## When Skirting Damage Is Really a Leveling Problem

Sometimes the skirting is only the symptom. A home that is settling or tilting pushes on the skirt from the inside. Panels get crushed on the low side and pulled out of the track on the high side. Replacing them just sets the new ones up to fail.

Check for these signs before you spend money on panels:

- Damage is worse on one side or one corner, not spread evenly.
- Panels are bowed outward or pinched at the top, with the top trim pulled off the home.
- Doors or windows stick, or a gap has opened at a corner where there was none.
- A level set on the floor shows the home sloping.

If you see two or more, get the home checked before you touch the skirting. ## Repair or Replace: Making the Call

Repair makes sense when:

- The damage is in a few spots, not across the whole perimeter.
- The panels away from the damage still flex without cracking.
- The track is straight and the home is level.
- You can still buy panels that match close enough, or your park does not care.

Replace when:

- Most panels are brittle, chalky, or cracking.
- The track is bent or rotted along most walls.
- You want to switch materials. Rigid-foam faux-stone skirting is the other option we install, and our post on [faux stone skirting for mobile homes](/blog/faux-stone-skirting-mobile-home/) compares it with vinyl.
- The repairs you have already made keep failing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I repair mobile home skirting myself?

Yes, for panel swaps and small track fixes. Most vinyl systems come apart without special tools. Check how your panels lock together first, cut the new panel to height, and leave the expansion gap. Call a crew if the damage runs along a whole wall, if panels keep failing in the same place, or if you suspect the home is moving.

### How much does mobile home skirting repair cost?

Our skirting repairs run $250-$800, depending on how many panels, how much track or trim, and how much digging and regrading the job needs. A full skirting install runs $1,200-$3,500. The written estimate is free.

### Do I have to replace all the skirting if a few panels are damaged?

No. If the damage is in a few spots and the rest of the vinyl still flexes without cracking, swapping the bad panels is enough. If most of the panels are brittle or the track is bent along most walls, replacing the run usually makes more sense than repairing it piece by piece.

### Why do my new skirting panels keep popping out or buckling?

Usually one of three things: the bottom track is bent or buried, the panel was installed without room to expand in the heat, or the home is settling and pushing on the skirt. If the damage is worse on one side of the home, have the level checked before you replace the panels again.
