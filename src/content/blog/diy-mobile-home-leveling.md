## What DIY Mobile Home Leveling Actually Requires

Start with what's under your home. A typical single wide in Marion County sits on 12 to 20 concrete block piers. A double wide can have 30 or more, plus piers along the marriage line where the two halves join. Each pier carries a share of the home's weight, and over time our sandy Central Florida soil shifts, washes, and compacts unevenly. Piers settle at different rates, the steel frame stops sitting flat, and you get sticking doors, sloped floors, and cracks over window corners.

Leveling means raising the low sections of the frame back to a common plane and resetting or shimming the piers underneath so they carry weight evenly again. That's it in one sentence. In practice, it means:

- Crawling the full length of the home, often through spider webs, insulation scraps, and whatever the last owner left under there
- Reading a water level or laser level accurately at every pier, to within about a quarter inch
- Jacking a multi-ton structure a fraction of an inch at a time without twisting the frame
- Placing hardwood shims and pier caps so the load transfers cleanly, not on a wedge edge that will crush later

If you've never done it, plan on a full weekend for a single wide and considerably more for a double wide. Pros do a single wide in a day because they've done hundreds. Your first one will not go like their five hundredth.

## Step by Step: How Leveling a Mobile Home Yourself Actually Goes

Assuming you've decided to try it, here's the sequence a careful DIYer follows. Read it once as a plan and a second time as a reality check.

**1. Inspect before you touch anything.** Crawl the full underside with a headlamp. Look for cracked blocks, tilted piers, rusted frame sections, sagging belly board, and soft wet soil. Mark every pier with chalk and note which ones have gaps between the cap and the frame. Our [underside inspection walkthrough](/blog/how-pros-check-mobile-home-level/) shows what pros look for at this stage.

**2. Establish your reference.** Run a water level along the main I-beams and record the reading at every pier. You're building a map of high and low spots. Most homes in our area are low at one end or along one side where rainwater has been washing soil away for years.

**3. Set your jack on a solid base near the lowest pier.** The jack goes under the steel I-beam, never under the floor, axles, plumbing, or the hitch. On Florida sand, the jack needs a pad or it will press itself into the ground instead of lifting the home.

**4. Lift slowly, a quarter inch at a time.** You'll hear the home creak and pop. That's the structure moving, and small moves are fine. Big fast lifts are how frames twist, drywall splits, and water lines pull apart at their fittings.

**5. Shim or rebuild the pier, then ease the weight back down.** Hardwood wedges go in pairs from opposite sides so the bearing surface stays flat. If a pier is cracked or leaning, it needs to be rebuilt from the footer up, not shimmed taller.

**6. Re-check every pier after every adjustment.** Lifting one spot changes the load on its neighbors. This is the step DIYers skip, and it's why so many DIY jobs are out of level again within months.

**7. Check your tie-downs when you're done.** Raising the frame changes strap tension. Florida law cares a lot about this, and so does your insurance company. The state's mobile home installation standards are administered by the [Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles](https://www.flhsmv.gov/motor-vehicles-tags-titles/mobile-homes/), and loose or rusted straps are a common 4-point inspection failure. If your straps look questionable, our [tie-down service page](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/) explains what proper anchoring involves.

## DIY vs. Professional Mobile Home Leveling: The Real Math

Money is usually the whole reason people consider DIY, so let's be straight about it.

A pro crew brings a truck full of 20-ton jacks, cribbing, blocks, caps, and hardwood shims. They level a single wide in two to five hours and warranty the work. You bring $350 to $800 in new tools, a free weekend, and a learning curve. If your home just needs routine shimming and your piers are sound, DIY can genuinely save you $200 to $500.

But the math flips fast when anything is nonstandard. Cracked piers, a twisted frame, soft soil, plumbing that's already tight, a double wide marriage line, or a home that hasn't been releveled in 15 years all raise the odds of the DIY job costing more than the pro job would have. HUD's [manufactured housing program](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/rmra/mhs/mhshome) sets the construction standards these homes are built to, and those standards assume the support system is installed and maintained correctly. When a DIY fix drifts from that, you can create inspection and insurance problems that follow the home for years.

There's also the timing question. If you're leveling because of a 4-point inspection deadline, a sale, or an insurance requirement, a DIY job usually won't come with the documentation the other party wants. Pros hand you paperwork. Your bottle jack doesn't.

## When to Put the Jack Down and Call a Professional

Some situations take DIY off the table completely. Stop and call a pro if you see any of these:

- **Cracked, crushed, or leaning piers.** These need rebuilding under load, which requires cribbing and equipment most homeowners don't have.
- **A double wide that's off along the marriage line.** Get this wrong and you damage the roof seam, ceiling, and both halves of the home.
- **Rusted or damaged frame steel.** Jacking against compromised steel is how frames buckle.
- **Soft, wet, or washing soil.** Leveling on bad ground fails within a year. Drainage or footer work comes first.
- **Signs of repeated settling.** If the home has needed leveling more than once in a few years, something underneath is wrong. Our post on [why mobile homes settle in Florida](/blog/why-mobile-homes-settle-florida/) covers the usual suspects.
- **Any moment the jack tilts, sinks, or the home shifts sideways.** Back out. No amount of saved money is worth being under a home that moves.

And a hard safety rule that applies to everyone, DIY or not: never put any part of your body under the frame unless the home is supported by cribbing or piers, not just the jack. Jacks fail. Hydraulics leak. Sand shifts. Every year people are killed under vehicles and structures held up by nothing but a jack, and a mobile home outweighs your truck several times over.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it legal to level my own mobile home in Florida?

Generally yes, an owner can perform maintenance like releveling on a home they own and occupy. The picture changes if the home is in a park with rules about who works under homes, or if the work involves the tie-down and anchoring system, which falls under state installation standards. When in doubt, check with your park manager and the Marion County building department before starting.

### How much does DIY mobile home leveling cost compared to hiring a pro?

Plan on $350 to $800 for proper tools and materials if you're starting from scratch. Professional releveling in the Ocala area typically runs $450 to $900 for a single wide and $700 to $1,500 for a double wide. If you already own good jacks and your piers are sound, DIY can save a few hundred dollars. If anything needs rebuilding, the pro route is usually cheaper in the end.

### What size jack do I need to level a mobile home?

Use a 12-ton bottle jack at absolute minimum, and a 20-ton is the safer choice under steel I-beams. Automotive scissor jacks and small floor jacks are dangerous under a structure this heavy. Just as important as the jack is what's under it: a solid steel or concrete pad, because Florida sand will swallow a jack base before the home ever lifts.

### How do I know if my home needs leveling or something bigger?

Sticking doors, new drywall cracks, and sloping floors point to routine settling that leveling fixes. Cracked or leaning piers, repeated settling within a couple of years, standing water underneath, or movement after heavy rain suggest a soil or drainage problem that leveling alone won't solve. A professional inspection sorts one from the other, and ours comes free with a written estimate.
