## Gear Up First: What You Need and When Not to Crawl

You don't need much. Grab these before you open a skirting panel:

- A bright flashlight or headlamp (headlamp is better, it frees both hands)
- Old long sleeves, pants, gloves, and something to lie on, like a scrap of cardboard
- Your phone, for photos of anything questionable
- A cheap torpedo level, if you have one
- A marble or golf ball for the quick floor test inside

Two safety rules, and we mean them. First, never crawl under a home that looks visibly unlevel, has piers that appear cracked or tipped, or shifted noticeably after a storm. A mobile home frame carries thousands of pounds per pier, and you don't get a warning when one lets go. Inspect that home from the skirting opening with a flashlight only, then call a professional.

Second, back out if you see a snake, heavy wasp activity, or strong mold smell. Wildlife loves the shade under Florida homes, and disturbed insulation can carry droppings you shouldn't breathe. Look, photograph from a distance, and leave the hands-on work to someone with protective gear.

Pick a dry morning. You'll see moisture problems more clearly when it hasn't rained in a day or two, because standing water at that point means drainage trouble, not just yesterday's storm.

## The 20-Minute Mobile Home Underside Inspection Checklist

Work in one direction, front to back, and give each zone about four minutes. Photograph anything you're unsure about so you can compare next year.

### Minutes 1 to 4: Piers and blocks

Sight down each row of piers like you're aiming down a fence line. They should stand straight and vertical. Flag any pier that leans, shows stair-step cracks in the block, or has visible gaps between the cap and the frame. Check for shims that have slipped, crushed, or fallen out. One leaning pier is a repair. Several leaning the same direction means the home is settling and needs [professional leveling](/mobile-home-leveling-ocala/) before doors and walls start telling you the same thing.

### Minutes 5 to 8: Anchors and straps

Follow each steel strap from the frame down to the ground anchor. Surface rust that wipes off is normal in Florida. Flaking, scaling rust, especially in the last few inches near the soil, is not. Tug each strap gently; it should be tight, not floppy. Look for anchor heads that have pulled up or tilted in the sand. Loose or corroded tie-downs are the number one 4-point inspection failure we see, and our post on [rusted mobile home anchors](/blog/rusted-mobile-home-anchors/) shows exactly what failing straps look like.

### Minutes 9 to 12: Belly board and insulation

Shine your light across the underside of the floor. The belly wrap (that black or gray fabric layer) should be smooth and tight. Flag sags that look like a hammock, tears with insulation hanging out, water stains, or dark drip lines under bathrooms and the kitchen. A sagging pocket usually means a plumbing leak is collecting above it.

### Minutes 13 to 16: Moisture, ground, and vapor barrier

Scan the ground itself. You're looking for standing water, damp channels where water flows through, white mineral crust, or a musty smell. Check that the ground-level [vapor barrier](/vapor-barrier-ocala/) plastic covers the soil without big gaps or ponding on top of it. If water is getting under the home regularly, fixing that drainage matters more than anything else on this list, and our guide to [water under a mobile home](/blog/water-under-mobile-home/) covers the usual fixes.

### Minutes 17 to 20: Skirting, vents, and pests

Circle the outside. Skirting panels should sit straight, with no buckling, gaps at the ground, or panels popped out of their track. Confirm vents are open and screened. Look for animal digging at the skirting base, droppings, or chewed insulation just inside the access panel. Then step inside and roll your marble across a few floors. If it takes off in one direction in multiple rooms, note which way. That's your early settling alarm.

Whole thing done? Write the date on your checklist and file your photos. You now know more about your home's condition than most owners ever do.

## Red Flags That Mean Stop and Call a Pro

Most of what you find on a yearly check is watch-and-wait material. These findings are not. Stop the DIY at this line:

- **Multiple piers leaning the same direction.** The home is moving as a unit. That's a leveling job, not a shim job.
- **Any crushed, cracked, or collapsed pier.** Stay out from under the home entirely.
- **Straps you can move by hand or anchors pulling out of the ground.** Your wind protection is compromised, and Florida's [tie-down standards](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/) exist for a reason.
- **Strong mold smell or visible black growth.** The EPA's [mold guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold) is blunt about not disturbing large mold areas without protection.
- **Sewage smell or raw water leaks.** Health hazard first, repair second.
- **New gaps between the home and its steps, or doors that suddenly won't latch.** Settling has reached the structure. Our post on [how often mobile homes need releveling](/blog/how-often-relevel-mobile-home/) explains what's normal and what's not.

Doug in Dunnellon hit the first bullet last July. His marble test rolled hard toward the back corner, and under the home, three piers leaned the same way after weeks of heavy rain. He called that week, and releveling ran him $680. The estimator's honest take: another wet season of movement and he'd have been looking at pier replacement and interior repairs closer to $4,200. ## Make It Stick: Log Your Underside Inspection Every Year

A single inspection tells you what's there. Two inspections a year apart tell you what's moving, and movement is what costs money.

Keep it simple. Create a note on your phone called "Under-home check" with three things: the date, your photos, and one line per zone (piers, straps, belly, moisture, skirting). Next spring, shoot the same photos from the same spots. A pier that leaned slightly in both photos is stable. A pier that leaned more this year is a phone call.

Two timing tips for Central Florida. Run your main check in spring, before the June-through-September rains, so you fix drainage and pier issues before the wet season tests them. And add a quick bonus check after any named storm or any week with flooding rain, because saturated sand is when piers and anchors actually move.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I inspect under my mobile home?

Once a year is the baseline, ideally in spring before Florida's rainy season. Add a quick 5-minute look after any hurricane, tropical storm, or week of flooding rain, since saturated soil is when piers settle and anchors shift. Homes over 20 years old or homes with past leveling work benefit from a second full check in the fall.

### Can I do this inspection myself, or do I need a professional?

The 20-minute visual check is safe for most owners as long as the home looks level and the piers look sound from the access panel. Never crawl under a home with cracked or leaning piers, and back out if you smell strong mold or see wildlife. A professional inspection makes sense before buying a home, before listing an insurance 4-point, or any time your DIY check turns up a red flag.

### What's the most common problem found under mobile homes in the Ocala area?

Moisture is the big one, and it drives most of the others. Poor drainage and missing vapor barriers keep the soil damp, which rusts anchor straps, softens the ground under piers, and feeds mold in the belly insulation. That's why the moisture zone of the checklist matters even when everything else looks fine.

### Will a good underside inspection help with my Florida homeowners insurance?

Indirectly, yes. Insurers on older mobile homes usually require a 4-point inspection, and tie-downs and underside condition are common failure points. Finding and fixing rusted straps or leaning piers before you apply means you pass on the first try instead of scrambling against a deadline. Keep your dated photos, since they help document the home's condition over time.
