Mobile home inspection cost depends on one question: which inspection are you being asked for? "Mobile home inspection" covers at least five different jobs, done by different people, for different reasons. A buyer's general inspection, an insurance 4-point, a tie-down letter, an engineer's foundation certification and an under-home leveling inspection do not overlap much, and they are not priced the same way.

We do two of the five. For those, the prices are on this page. For the other three, the inspector or engineer sets the fee, so we won't guess at it. Get that number in writing before anyone drives out.

## The Five Inspections at a Glance

| Inspection | Who does it | Who usually asks |
|---|---|---|
| General home inspection | Florida-licensed home inspector | Buyer, sometimes a lender |
| 4-point inspection | Licensed inspector | Insurance company |
| Tie-down certification letter | Us | Insurance company, lender, park |
| FHA foundation certification | Florida-licensed engineer | Lender on an FHA-backed loan |
| Under-home leveling inspection | Us | Owner, buyer or seller |

Most people who search for a manufactured home inspection are really in one of these five boxes. Find yours below.

## 1. General Home Inspection

This is the broad walk-through a buyer orders before closing on a used home. A licensed home inspector looks at the roof, the exterior, the interior, the electrical panel, plumbing, water heater, HVAC, windows and doors, and writes up what they see. In Florida, home inspectors are licensed by the state, and you can ask to see the license before you book.

Two limits apply. First, a general inspector often looks at the underside of a manufactured home from the access panel or the skirting, not from under the home. Second, the report is a snapshot of condition. It is not a certification that the home meets any standard.

The fee is whatever that inspector charges, and it varies with the size of the home and the inspector. Ask what is included, whether the underside is crawled or just viewed, and how soon you get the written report.

If you are buying used, the buyer's walk-through has its own list: [the used mobile home inspection checklist](/blog/used-mobile-home-inspection-checklist/). We won't repeat it here.

## 2. The 4-Point Inspection for Insurance

A 4-point inspection looks at four systems only: roof, electrical, plumbing and HVAC. Insurers ask for it on older homes before they write or renew a policy. It is short, it is narrow, and it says nothing about whether your home is level or properly anchored.

The inspector is a licensed professional, and the fee is theirs to set. Call two or three and ask for the price up front. If the report flags something, you can fix it, but ask about re-inspection terms before you pay.

We have a full guide on what gets checked and what mobile homes tend to fail on: [the 4-point inspection for mobile homes](/blog/four-point-inspection-mobile-home/).

One thing people mix up: a 4-point report does not satisfy a request for tie-down proof. If your insurer wants both, you need two different documents.

## 3. The Tie-Down Certification Letter

Some insurers, lenders and parks want written proof that your home is anchored. That is a tie-down certification letter. Someone qualified goes under the home, checks the anchors, straps and ties, and writes a letter saying what they found.

We do these. A tie-down certification letter runs $150-$300. If the anchors check out, the letter is what you hand over. If they don't, the fix is a separate job, and the written estimate for it is free. A full tie-down or anchor package, when a home needs one, is $1,500-$3,000.

The letter exists because of the federal installation rules, which say that after blocking and leveling, a manufactured home must be secured against the wind, either with anchor assembly type installations or by connecting it to an alternative foundation system ([24 CFR 3285.401](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.401)). A letter is the paper that shows yours still is.

Our [tie-down certification and insurance guide](/blog/tie-down-certification-insurance/) explains how to ask your insurer for exactly what they need, so you don't pay for a letter they won't accept.

## 4. The FHA and Engineer Foundation Certification

This one is different in kind. When a buyer uses an FHA-backed loan on a manufactured home, the lender may ask for a certification that the foundation meets the program's requirements. That document comes from a Florida-licensed engineer, not from an installer, a home inspector or us.

We don't write engineering letters, and we won't pretend a tie-down letter can stand in for one. What we can do is look at the piers, anchors and level ahead of time, so you know what the engineer is likely to find and can fix it before the engineer arrives.

The engineer sets the fee. Ask for it in writing, ask what site visit is included, and ask what happens if the foundation needs work before they will sign. The full process is in [the FHA foundation certification guide](/blog/fha-foundation-certification-mobile-home/).

Lenders and engineers do not like surprises in closing week. If you are a seller whose buyer is using FHA financing, find out early whether a certification is required and who the lender wants to sign it.

## 5. The Under-Home Leveling Inspection

This is the one nobody else on the list covers. A leveling inspection is a written look at what is under your home: pier condition, footings, shims, spacing, how the frame reads across its length and width, and whether the home is still level.

An inspection is not an estimate. Our leveling inspection costs $150-$250 and comes with a written report. Only the estimate is free. If the report shows the home needs releveling, we write you a free estimate for the work, and the price ranges for that are on [our mobile home leveling cost page](/mobile-home-leveling-cost/).

What the written report is for:

- A buyer who wants to know what the underside looks like before closing.
- A seller who wants a clear answer before listing, so a buyer's inspector doesn't raise it first.
- An owner who sees sticking doors, cracks over the doors and windows, or bouncy floors and wants to know why.
- Anyone a lender or insurer asks for evidence of the home's condition and level, though they may still require their own forms.

Here is what we look at underneath:

- Every pier sitting on a footing. The federal rule is that a footing must support every pier, set on undisturbed soil or fill compacted to 90 percent of maximum relative density ([24 CFR 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312)).
- Pier height, spacing and alignment, and whether any have tilted or sunk.
- Shims that have slipped, cracked or crushed.
- Anchors and straps, as far as rust and slack are visible.
- The vapor barrier and the ground beneath it, including standing water.
- The marriage line on a double-wide.

If the report turns up work, the typical price ranges are $300-$600 for a single corner releveled, $450-$900 for a full single-wide relevel, and $700-$1,500 for a full double-wide relevel. The written estimate gives you the real number for your home.

## What About "Manufactured Home Inspection Cost"?

People search "manufactured home inspection cost" expecting one number. There isn't one, because the term covers all five jobs above. The answer is a sequence of questions:

1. Who is asking for the inspection: a buyer, an insurer, a lender or a park?
2. What exactly do they want in writing: a condition report, a 4-point form, anchor proof or an engineer's certification?
3. Who is qualified to produce that document?
4. What does that person charge, in writing, and what is included?

Our two numbers are $150-$250 for the leveling inspection and $150-$300 for the tie-down certification letter. For a general inspector, a 4-point inspector or an engineer, call and ask. Prices change with the size of the home, the distance and the inspector, and a number you read online is never a quote.

## Common Ways People Pay for the Wrong Document

Ordering the wrong inspection. An insurer asks for "an inspection" and the owner books a general home inspection when the insurer wanted a 4-point or a tie-down letter. Ask for the form name, or ask the agent to email you the requirement.

Paying twice for the same look. If a general inspector, a 4-point inspector and a tie-down crew each go over the same home in the same month, you may be paying three times for overlapping access. Ask whether one visit can produce more than one document, and whether that person is qualified to sign each of them.

Skipping the underside. Many of the things that cost buyers money, such as sagging, sunken piers, loose anchors and moisture, live below the floor. If your inspector only views the underside from an access panel, a separate underside look may be worth having. Our [underside inspection checklist](/blog/mobile-home-underside-inspection-checklist/) shows what that involves.

Accepting an unlicensed signature on a licensed job. A certification that has to come from a Florida-licensed engineer is only worth something if it does. Ask for the name and license number, and check it yourself.

Fixing before inspecting. Releveling a home on a bad footing, or re-strapping rusted anchors, before anyone has looked under it just buries the cause. Inspect first, then price the repair.

## Which One Should You Book First?

Use the reason you are being asked:

- Buying a used home: a general inspection, plus an under-home leveling inspection if you want a written read on the piers and level.
- Insurance renewal on an older home: the 4-point inspection, and the tie-down letter if the insurer asks for anchor proof.
- FHA-backed loan: the engineer's foundation certification, with a look under the home first.
- Doors sticking or floors out of level: the leveling inspection. You don't need a general inspection to find a pier that has sunk.
- Selling: a leveling inspection, and the tie-down letter if the buyer's insurer wants one.

If you are not sure, say so. Call us and describe who is asking for what. We will tell you whether it is one of ours or someone else's, and we won't sell you a document the other party won't accept.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a mobile home inspection cost?

It depends on which inspection you mean. Our under-home leveling inspection is $150-$250 with a written report, and our tie-down certification letter is $150-$300. A general home inspector, a 4-point inspector and an engineer each set their own fee, so ask for it in writing before they come out.

### Is a manufactured home inspection different from a regular home inspection?

The process is similar, but the home is not. A manufactured home sits on piers and anchors rather than a slab or stem wall, and it carries a data plate and HUD labels. A good inspection looks under the home, not just inside it, and covers the piers, anchors and belly. If your inspector only views the underside from an access panel, ask whether a separate underside look is worth it.

### Does a 4-point inspection tell me if my mobile home is level?

No. A 4-point inspection covers the roof, electrical, plumbing and HVAC, and it is meant for insurance. It says nothing about piers, anchors or level. For that you need a leveling inspection, and for anchor proof you need a tie-down certification letter.

### Do you do FHA foundation certifications?

No. That certification has to come from a Florida-licensed engineer, and we don't write engineering letters. We can inspect the piers, anchors and level beforehand so you know what to fix before the engineer visits, and we do the repair work if it is needed.
