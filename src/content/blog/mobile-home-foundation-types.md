A mobile home foundation is whatever carries the home's weight into the ground. In Marion County that is usually a grid of stacked piers sitting on footings under the steel frame, not a poured basement or a full slab. Other setups exist: concrete runners, a full slab and an engineered permanent foundation. Anchors and stabilizers get a section too, since people often mistake them for a foundation.

This guide covers each type: what it is, what it means for leveling and tie-downs, and who builds it. We level and tie down homes. We do not pour concrete or build permanent foundations, and we will say so each time it matters.

## What a mobile home foundation has to do

Federal installation rules say a foundation must be designed and built for the site conditions, the home's design, and the loads shown on its data plate ([24 CFR 3285.301](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.301)). The same section allows systems that are not the usual pier-and-footing type, but only when an engineer's data backs them up.

That one rule explains why there are several foundation types. The home does not care which one it sits on, as long as the loads reach firm ground without moving.

Every type also has to leave room under the home. The rules call for at least 12 inches between the bottom of the main frame beam and the grade under the whole home ([24 CFR 3285.305](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.305)). That gap is how we get under a home to level it, inspect it, or fix a pier.

## Dry-stacked piers on footings

This is the setup under most homes we work on in Ocala and the rest of Marion County. It is what people usually mean by a mobile home pier foundation.

**What it is.** Rows of piers run under the steel frame. A pier is a stack of load-bearing concrete blocks, a pressure-treated wood pier, or an adjustable metal or concrete pier. Blocks are stacked with the hollow cells running vertically, capped, and the gap between the cap and the frame is filled with shims driven in tight. Every pier has to sit on its own footing ([24 CFR 3285.303 and 3285.304](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.304)).

**Footings.** The footing is the pad under the pier. The rules allow precast concrete pads, poured concrete pads, pressure-treated wood, listed ABS plastic pads, and other listed materials, all set on undisturbed soil or compacted fill ([24 CFR 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312)). We explain pad sizes and materials in [our guide to mobile home pier pads](/blog/mobile-home-pier-pads/).

**Leveling.** This type is the easiest to adjust. Because the piers are stacked and shimmed, a crew can jack the frame, add or swap shims, replace a failed pier, or reset a footing that has sunk. Florida's sandy soil and summer rain make that a normal part of owning a home on piers. Our [mobile home leveling service in Ocala](/mobile-home-leveling-ocala/) covers exactly this setup.

**Tie-downs.** Piers carry weight, not wind loads. Ground anchors and straps hold the home against wind, and they are a separate system.

**Who builds it.** The installer who sets the home, licensed through the state. The footings and piers go in during set-up, and a leveling company like ours adjusts and repairs them afterward.

## Concrete runners and ribbon footings

Concrete runners, sometimes called ribbons, are long strips of poured concrete running under the lines where piers would sit. You will see them under older homes, and on lots where someone wanted a continuous footing.

**What it is.** Instead of one pad per pier, a runner is a continuous footing. Piers or blocking still stand on top of it and still carry the frame. The federal footing rule lists poured-in-place pads, slabs, or ribbons as acceptable footing types, with a minimum thickness and concrete strength ([24 CFR 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312)). So a runner is just another kind of footing under the same piers.

**Leveling.** Runners can crack or settle just like pads, and when one end drops, the piers above it drop with it. We can often level over a runner by adjusting the piers and shims on top. If the runner itself has failed, a concrete or foundation contractor needs to repair or replace it, and for a structural question an engineer should look at it. We do not do that concrete work.

**Tie-downs.** Anchors usually still go into the ground beside the runner, not through it. Where they go depends on the installation instructions and the home's anchoring plan.

**Who builds it.** A concrete contractor, or the installer working with one. If you are searching for concrete runners for a mobile home, ask for a contractor who has built under manufactured homes before, and ask whether the design follows the home's manufacturer instructions.

## A full concrete slab (the mobile home pad)

When people search for a mobile home pad, they mean one of two things: a graded dirt or gravel pad in a park, or a poured concrete slab. The first is where the home sits; the second is a foundation choice.

**What it is.** A poured slab under the whole footprint, or under part of it, with piers or blocking resting on it. The federal rule recognizes slabs as footings. A slab used as a footing still has to meet the strength and thickness the rule sets, and design loads may call for reinforcing steel ([24 CFR 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312)).

**Leveling.** A slab gives the piers a flat, hard base, so the home tends to stay level where a pad sits on bare sand might not. It does not make leveling a one-time job. Slabs can still crack, and the frame can still move on its piers. Water that gets under the home can undermine the edges.

**Tie-downs.** Anchoring a home on a slab is a different job from anchoring one on soil. Anchors have to be rated for the slab or set in the ground beyond it, according to the installation instructions. This is a question for the installer and, where the design is custom, an engineer.

**Who builds it.** A concrete contractor, usually with a permit from the county or city. We do not pour slabs, and we will not guess at whether a particular slab is adequate for your home.

## Permanent foundations

A permanent foundation is a foundation built to a specific engineered design, usually with the home tied to it so it works as one structure. Lenders sometimes require one, which is why it comes up in buying, selling, and refinancing.

**What it is.** The design depends on the program. A federally insured loan may require a foundation that meets HUD's guide and an engineer's certification that the home is on it. The rules also let a foundation not be a pier-and-footing type when an engineer's data supports it ([24 CFR 3285.301(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.301)). We cover how this lines up with piers in [permanent foundation vs piers](/blog/permanent-foundation-vs-piers/), and the lender side in [our post on FHA foundation certification](/blog/fha-foundation-certification-mobile-home/).

**Leveling.** A true permanent foundation is built to hold the home steady, so routine releveling is less of a recurring need. Problems still show up: cracks in walls, doors that stick, a corner that dropped. When they do, the question is usually whether the foundation or the home is moving, and that is an engineering question first.

**Tie-downs.** The tie-down method is part of the foundation design. It is not something to add on later without checking the plans.

**Who builds it.** A contractor working from drawings by a Florida-licensed engineer. We do not build permanent foundations and we do not write engineering letters. If a lender asks for one, we point you to an engineer, and we can describe what we see under the home so you know what to ask.

## Ground anchors and stabilizer systems

Ground anchors, straps, and stabilizer plates are the wind-resistance system. They are not what carries the home's weight.

**What it is.** Anchors screw or drive into the soil, and steel straps run from the anchor up to the frame, or over the top of the home. Stabilizer plates and similar hardware add resistance to sideways movement. Marion County is in HUD Wind Zone II, which affects how the system is sized.

**Leveling.** Anchors and piers work together. If a pier is adjusted without checking the straps, a strap that was snug can go slack, or one that was loose can pull hard. We check both on every leveling visit.

**Tie-downs.** This is the tie-down system. We install and replace anchor and strap packages, and we write tie-down certification letters. Details are on our [tie-down and anchor page](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/).

**Who builds it.** An installer, or a tie-down contractor like us for repairs and replacements.

## Which foundation does your home have

Look under the home. A flashlight and a few minutes will tell you most of it.

- Rows of stacked blocks or metal piers on flat pads: a pier-and-footing foundation. This is the common one.
- A continuous strip of concrete under the piers: runners or ribbons.
- Concrete across the whole ground area under the home: a slab.
- Concrete or block walls around the perimeter, with engineering paperwork on file: possibly a permanent foundation. The paperwork matters more than the look.
- Steel straps rising from the ground to the frame: ground anchors. Every type of foundation on this list should still have them.

If you cannot tell, a leveling inspection is the quickest way to know. We look at the piers, footings, anchors, and clearance, and we tell you what we see. If the answer is a slab or footing problem, we will say that and refer you out rather than level over it.

Park homes add one more variable. The park may own the pad and set rules for who can work on it. Check your lease before hiring anyone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common mobile home foundation in Florida?

Dry-stacked piers on footings are what we see under most Marion County homes. Each pier sits on its own footing, and the frame rests on the piers. Slabs, runners, and engineered permanent foundations exist, but they are less common on homes that were set in a park or on a rural lot.

### Is a concrete slab a better mobile home foundation than piers?

Not automatically. A slab gives the piers a firm, flat base, but it can still crack, and water can still wash out its edges. Piers on well-set footings are a standard, accepted way to support a home. The right answer depends on the soil, the home, and what your lender or park requires.

### Do concrete runners for a mobile home replace piers?

No. A runner is a continuous footing, and piers or blocking still sit on top of it. Federal rules list poured ribbons as one allowed type of footing. If a runner cracks or sinks, the piers above it move too.

### Can you pour a foundation or build a permanent foundation for my home?

No. We do not do concrete work or permanent foundations. We level homes, repair and replace piers, and handle tie-downs and skirting. For a new foundation or an engineering letter, hire a concrete or foundation contractor and a Florida-licensed engineer.
