A truck backs a new or used manufactured home onto your lot, and then what? Mobile home set up in Florida runs through a set sequence: permit, delivery, blocking, leveling, anchoring, utility hookups, and skirting, most of it finished before you ever get a key. If nobody walks you through that order, it's easy to sign a set-up package without knowing what you actually paid for.

This article walks through that sequence the way Florida requires it, who is typically responsible for each piece, and the questions to ask before you sign anything. It also covers the parts of that process owners call us about once the crew has left: releveling, tie-downs and certification letters, skirting, and vapor barrier, plus what a level check after your first wet season should look like.

Think of it as a plain explanation of what's supposed to happen, who's supposed to do it, and what to check once it's done.

## Site Prep, Delivery, Blocking, and Leveling

Before a home ever leaves the dealer's lot, the site has to be ready and permitted. In Marion County that means a building permit and inspection from the county or city building department, along with a pad that's graded, cleared, and accessible for delivery equipment.

Delivery comes next. Each section (a single-wide is one, a double-wide is two) arrives on its own running gear and gets positioned on the pad. From there the installer takes over the part most owners never see: blocking and leveling.

Every section rests on a grid of footings and piers set out according to the manufacturer's installation manual, which specifies footing size, pier spacing, and pier height. Federal installation standards require every pier to sit on its own footing, set on undisturbed soil or compacted fill ([24 CFR § 3285.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3285.312)). We wrote a full breakdown of how that grid works in [mobile home pier pads and footings](/blog/mobile-home-pier-pads/). The installer jacks each section to the height the manual calls for, sets footings on prepared ground, stacks piers, and shims each one until the section reads level.

On a double-wide, there's an extra step: joining the marriage line where the two halves meet. The sections have to be brought together, aligned, and fastened along that seam before the home is structurally one unit, and the marriage line needs its own supports under the points where the two halves carry concentrated load. We cover the differences between single-wide and double-wide leveling, including why the marriage line matters, in [leveling a single wide vs a double wide](/blog/leveling-single-wide-vs-double-wide/).

Only once every section is blocked, leveled, and joined does the home move to anchoring.

## Anchoring, Utilities, and Skirting

Marion County sits in HUD Wind Zone II, so the home has to be anchored to resist the wind loads for that zone, not a lighter one. That means a full set of ground anchors, straps, and frame or over the top ties, sized and spaced to the home's anchoring plan. We've written separately about what [Wind Zone II actually requires in Marion County](/blog/florida-wind-zones-mobile-homes/) and the specifics of [Florida's tie-down requirements](/blog/florida-mobile-home-tie-down-requirements/).

Anchoring at initial set-up is part of the installer's scope. Later on, if anchors have rusted, loosened, or a lender or insurer wants a current certification, that's a separate inspection and a separate letter, which is one of the pieces of set-up-adjacent work we price. Our [tie-down and anchor service in Ocala](/mobile-home-tie-downs-ocala/) covers that side of it.

Utility hookups run alongside or right after anchoring: electrical, water, sewer or septic, and gas where it applies. These are separate licensed trades from the mobile home installer, and their work may be permitted and inspected separately, which is one reason a set-up package can involve more than one crew showing up over more than one visit.

Skirting usually goes on last, once utilities are connected and the crawl space is otherwise finished. It has to include ventilation openings sized to the home's floor area, placed on at least two opposite sides for cross-ventilation, plus at least one access opening large enough to reach the utility connections underneath. We break down exactly how many vents a given home needs and where they go in [mobile home skirting vents](/blog/mobile-home-skirting-vents/).

Steps, the AC unit, and any remaining exterior work typically wrap up around the same time, and the job closes with a final inspection from the local building department before the home is considered done.

## Who Handles What

Florida requires that blocking, leveling, and anchoring a newly delivered home be done by an installer licensed through the Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles (DHSMV), under the state's installation rule, Rule 15C-1. In practice, the dealer you bought the home from usually arranges that installer as part of the sale, and the set-up line on your contract covers the installer's work.

Utility hookups are a separate matter. Electrical, water, sewer or septic, and gas connections go to the licensed trade for that utility, not the mobile home installer, and each may need its own permit and inspection.

If you're placing the home in a manufactured home park or 55+ community, ask the park office what it requires before the crew shows up. Parks often have their own rules about skirting material, setback from the lot line, or who's allowed to do utility work inside the park, on top of what the county requires. We cover how that layered responsibility plays out for repairs in [mobile home park repair responsibility in Florida](/blog/mobile-home-park-repair-responsibility/), and the same idea applies at set-up.

Whoever does the blocking, leveling, and anchoring, ask for their name and DHSMV installer license number before they start, and take a few minutes to verify it yourself with DHSMV. ## Questions to Ask Before You Sign the Set-Up Package

A set-up quote can look like one number that actually bundles a dozen line items, some of which might be missing. Before you sign, get an answer in writing on each of these:

- Is the building permit and inspection included in the price, or billed separately?
- What footings and piers is the installer using, and are they sized to your home's installation manual?
- How many anchors will be installed, and what type: auger, concrete deadman, or another kind?
- Is skirting included, and if so, which material?
- Is a ground vapor barrier included under the home?
- Are steps included at each exterior door?
- Is the AC unit set and connected as part of this price?
- Are utility runs and any trenching included, or billed separately?
- Who removes packaging, blocking scraps, and other debris when the job is done?
- Will you get copies of the installation and anchoring paperwork when it's finished?

That last one matters more than it sounds. You'll want that paperwork later, for insurance, for a tie-down certification letter if one's ever requested, and for a 4-point inspection if you sell the home. Ask for it in writing before you sign, not after the crew has already packed up.

## After the Crew Leaves: Watch the First Wet Season

A freshly set home sits on freshly disturbed ground. Even when footings are placed on properly compacted soil, the fill around and under a new pad keeps settling for a while, and Marion County's sandy soil shifts once it's saturated. Central Florida's wet season, roughly June through September, is the first real test a new set-up gets. Have the home's level checked after that first summer, before small settling turns into sticking doors or a sloped floor. Our [mobile home leveling service in Ocala](/mobile-home-leveling-ocala/) covers that check, and we've written more about the pattern in [why homes settle after heavy rain](/blog/mobile-home-settling-after-rain/).

While you're waiting out that first season, take care of two pieces of paperwork most owners forget about. Every manufactured home carries a data plate, usually inside a kitchen cabinet, a bedroom closet, or near the electrical panel, listing the home's serial number, its wind and roof load zone, and other build details ([24 CFR § 3280.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-3280.5)). Each section also carries a separate HUD certification label, a small metal tag riveted to the outside at the taillight end, the end opposite the tow bar. Photograph both now, while you know exactly where they are. We explain what each one tells you, and why you want a photo on file, in [the HUD data plate on a mobile home](/blog/mobile-home-hud-data-plate/).

Ask your installer for copies of the blocking, leveling, and anchoring paperwork too, and keep it with the data plate photos. You'll want all of it together the next time anyone asks for proof of how the home was set.

## Moving a Used Home Onto a New Lot

Buying a used mobile or manufactured home and moving it onto a new lot doesn't skip any of this. The home still needs a permit, still gets blocked and leveled on footings and piers sized to its installation manual, still gets anchored, and still needs utilities reconnected and skirting installed at the new site. The fact that the home has lived somewhere else before doesn't lower the standard.

Check one thing before you move a used home onto Marion County land: its data plate should show it was built for Wind Zone II, or a higher zone. A home built for Wind Zone I wasn't designed for Zone II wind loads, installers and insurers look for that on the plate, and it's a problem a new set-up alone won't solve. Check the data plate before the move, not after.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is included in a mobile home set-up?

A full set-up covers site prep and the building permit, delivery, blocking and leveling each section on footings and piers, joining the marriage line on a double-wide, anchoring to current wind zone requirements, utility hookups by the licensed trades, and skirting with the required vents and access opening. Steps, the AC unit, and a final building inspection typically close out the job. Exactly what's bundled into one price varies by dealer and installer, so get a written breakdown before you sign.

### Who is allowed to set up a mobile home in Florida?

Florida requires that blocking, leveling, and anchoring a newly delivered home be performed by a mobile home installer licensed through the Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles, under the state's installation rule. Utility connections are handled by the licensed trade for that utility, separate from the installer. Always ask for the installer's name and license number and verify it yourself before work begins.

### How soon after set-up should a mobile home be releveled?

There's no fixed schedule, since it depends on the site and how the soil settles. In Marion County's sandy soil, it's sensible to have the level checked after the home's first wet season, roughly the first June through September it's been in place, since that's when fresh fill and disturbed ground move the most. After that, a periodic check is reasonable, especially following an unusually wet summer.

### Does a used mobile home need a new set-up when it's moved to a new lot?

Yes. A used home moved to a new lot goes through the same site prep, permit, blocking, leveling, anchoring, utility, and skirting steps as a new one. Check the home's data plate before the move too, to confirm it was built for Wind Zone II, since Marion County is Wind Zone II no matter where the home lived before.
